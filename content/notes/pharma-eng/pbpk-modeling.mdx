Every model covered elsewhere in this pillar treats the body as abstract
boxes — a "central compartment," a "peripheral compartment" — that a drug
flows between according to fitted equations. It works, and it predicts
clinical data well, but it's abstract: you can't point to the peripheral
compartment on an anatomy diagram.

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling does something
different. You build a model that maps directly onto real anatomy — the
lungs, liver, kidneys, gut, muscle, fat, brain, heart — each as its own
compartment, carrying real measured physiology: blood flow to that organ,
enzyme activity there, plasma protein binding, transporter expression at
the gut wall. Then you put a candidate drug into this virtual body,
described only by its own physical and chemical properties — molecular
weight, lipophilicity, protein binding — and simulate where it goes and how
it's cleared, without having run a single clinical study yet.

That's what makes PBPK unusually useful: it can predict drug behaviour in
situations you have no data for at all — a paediatric patient, someone with
kidney failure, a different ethnic population, a second drug on board — all
from first principles.

## Where it sits in development

PBPK is the one modelling approach that genuinely spans the whole
pipeline: predicting human PK from lab data before any human trial in
discovery, translating animal data into a first-in-human dose,
predicting PK in special populations in Phase I to avoid running a
dedicated study for each one, and supporting or replacing clinical
drug-drug interaction studies in later development. The regulatory
significance here is real — the FDA now explicitly accepts PBPK model
predictions in place of certain clinical studies, which is a mathematical
model substituting for a trial, not just informing one.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

The core PBPK theory — perfusion-limited and permeability-limited organ
models — dates to the 1970s, and small-molecule PBPK for adults is
commercially mature, implemented in established software (Simcyp,
GastroPlus, the open-source PK-Sim). What's still frontier:

- **Pediatric PBPK** has to model organ growth, enzyme maturation, and
  changing body composition as a child develops — current models
  approximate this, with the biggest gaps in neonates and infants.
- **Biologic PBPK** — modelling FcRn-mediated recycling and
  target-mediated tissue distribution for antibodies — is still evolving;
  the mechanisms differ enough from small molecules that the standard PBPK
  toolkit doesn't transfer cleanly.
- **mRNA and gene therapy PBPK** is close to undeveloped: how a lipid
  nanoparticle distributes, crosses into a cell, escapes the endosome, and
  behaves intracellularly doesn't have a validated modelling framework
  yet.
- **Tumour PBPK** — how a drug actually distributes inside a solid
  tumour, given uneven vasculature, elevated internal pressure, and
  necrotic regions — is an active oncology-adjacent frontier.
- **Drug-disease interactions**: liver cirrhosis, heart failure, obesity,
  and inflammation all change the physiological parameters PBPK models
  normally treat as fixed, and most drugs are labelled from healthy-adult
  data even though the patients who need them often aren't healthy adults.

Software licence costs and specific market-size figures for any of this
change often enough that I'm not going to quote one here without a source
