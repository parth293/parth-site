A drug is "linear" when its behaviour scales predictably with dose: double
the dose, double the concentration in blood, because the body clears it at
a constant rate — water through a fixed pipe. Most drugs, at normal doses,
work this way, and the maths describing them is simple, clean exponentials.

Some drugs break that assumption, because the enzymes metabolising them
saturate. Once those enzymes are working at full capacity, more drug
doesn't mean faster clearance — it just piles up disproportionately. This
is nonlinear PK, and the standard maths for it — Michaelis-Menten
kinetics — is the same maths used to describe saturating enzyme behaviour
in biochemistry generally.

A few other routes into nonlinearity, beyond enzyme saturation:

- **Saturable protein binding** — a drug binds blood proteins that have
  limited capacity; past that limit, more of the drug is free and active.
- **Saturable transport** — the transporters that move a drug into cells
  get overwhelmed at high concentration.
- **Target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD)** — common in biologics: the
  drug binds its target so tightly and specifically that the target itself
  becomes a major route of clearance.
- **Auto-induction or auto-inhibition** — the drug changes the activity of
  the enzymes that metabolise it, over time, altering its own PK as
  treatment continues.

Phenytoin (epilepsy), warfarin (anticoagulation), and most monoclonal
antibodies are nonlinear. Dosing a nonlinear drug as if it were linear —
assuming the safe, well-tolerated response at a low dose will scale
smoothly to a higher one — is a real way to hurt someone.

## Where it sits in development

Nonlinear PK modelling gets called in wherever a drug's behaviour looks
unusual, rather than as a routine step every drug goes through. It matters
most acutely during Phase I dose escalation — the step where a trial moves
from a safe low dose toward a therapeutic one — because an undetected
nonlinearity is exactly what makes that transition dangerous.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

Michaelis-Menten kinetics for small molecules has been textbook material
since the 1970s — phenytoin and ethanol are the standard teaching examples.
What's genuinely still being built:

- **TMDD models for next-generation biologics** — bispecific and
  trispecific antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates — where the existing
  models, built for simple single-target antibodies, don't hold once a
  drug binds two targets at once.
- **Gene and cell therapy PK** is close to a blank page: how a viral vector
  distributes through the body, gets taken up by cells, and is cleared
  doesn't have an established modelling tradition to draw on yet.
- **Predicting nonlinearity onset ahead of time** — at what dose does a
  given drug stop behaving linearly — is currently mostly discovered after
  the fact, in a trial, rather than predicted beforehand from the
  molecule's properties.
- **Nonlinear PK in sick patients** — inflammation, organ failure, a large
  tumour burden all plausibly change where saturation kicks in, but the
  patients most likely to need the drug are exactly the ones usually
  excluded from the studies that build these models.
