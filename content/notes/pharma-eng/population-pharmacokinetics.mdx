Give the same drug, at the same dose, to a hundred different people, and
you'll get close to a hundred different responses. One person clears it in
four hours; another takes twelve. One gets the intended effect; a third
gets toxicity at the same dose that did nothing for the first two. Age,
weight, kidney function, genetics, diet, other medications — all of it
moves the number.

Classical pharmacokinetics was built to characterise one patient at a time,
intensively: many blood draws, controlled conditions. That tells you almost
nothing about the hundred-person spread above. Population pharmacokinetics
(PopPK) flips the approach — sparse data (a few blood samples per person,
at irregular real-world times) across many patients, fed into a statistical
model that separates out three things at once: the typical drug behaviour
across the population, how much individuals vary around that typical
behaviour, and which patient characteristics — age, renal function, a
genetic variant — actually explain that variation.

The mathematical engine behind this is nonlinear mixed-effects modelling
(NLME); the software that's been the industry standard since the 1970s is
called NONMEM.

## Where it sits in development

PopPK is unusual among PK/PD subfields in that it's used across the entire
pipeline, not just one stage — animal PopPK projecting human PK in
preclinical work, sparse-sampling PopPK in Phase I and II identifying which
covariates matter, large-scale PopPK in Phase III supporting the dose that
actually gets approved, and a PopPK report as a standard, expected
component of every NDA/BLA regulatory submission.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

The statistical methodology — NLME modelling, covariate analysis, the
regulatory expectations around a PopPK report — is mature; it's been the
standard approach for decades. What's still actively moving:

- **Biologics.** Monoclonal antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, and
  fusion proteins behave very differently from small molecules, and the
  models built for small-molecule PK don't extrapolate cleanly.
- **Novel modalities** — mRNA and gene therapy PK — are close to uncharted;
  there isn't an established modelling tradition to lean on yet.
- **Machine learning integration** — using ML to find which covariates
  matter in high-dimensional genomic or proteomic data, rather than testing
  them one at a time by hand — is an active area, not a settled one.
- **Pediatric and neonatal PopPK** is a real regulatory gap: children
  aren't small adults, and developmental physiology changes the picture in
  ways adult-derived models don't capture.
- **Real-world-data PopPK**, built from electronic health records instead
  of clean trial data, is genuinely harder — real-world data is messy in
  ways clinical trial data by design isn't — and still being worked out.

I don't have a reliable figure for how much of the field is "mature" versus
"frontier" — any such split is someone's rough characterisation, mine
included, not a measured statistic.
