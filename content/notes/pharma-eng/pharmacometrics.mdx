Each of the other PK/PD notes in this pillar answers one specific
question. [ADME](/notes/pharma-eng/adme) asks what the body does to a drug
physically.
[Population pharmacokinetics](/notes/pharma-eng/population-pharmacokinetics)
asks why the same dose behaves differently across people.
[Nonlinear PK](/notes/pharma-eng/nonlinear-pk-modeling) asks what happens
once the body's drug-handling systems saturate.
[DDI](/notes/pharma-eng/drug-drug-interactions) asks how one drug changes
what another does. [PBPK](/notes/pharma-eng/pbpk-modeling) asks whether all
of that can be simulated in a body built from real anatomy.

Pharmacometrics isn't another item on that list — it's the discipline that
uses all of them together to answer the harder question underneath: given
everything known about how a drug behaves, what dose, for which patients,
at what stage of their disease, maximises benefit and minimises harm. A
pharmacometrician builds models that connect drug concentration in the body
(the PK side, using PopPK, PBPK, and nonlinear-PK tools as needed), drug
effect on the body (the PD side — receptor occupancy, biomarker response,
disease progression), patient variability, and clinical outcome — and the
output isn't a graph, it's a quantitative basis for a real decision: which
dose goes into Phase III, whether a trial can be shortened, what the label
should say for children.

## Where it sits in development

Pharmacometrics is present at every stage of drug development, in a way no
other discipline covered in this pillar is: exposure-response modelling
selecting compounds in discovery, dose-escalation support in Phase I,
go/no-go and dose-selection recommendations from Phase II data, trial
simulation and adaptive design support in Phase III, and mandatory PopPK
and exposure-response reports in the regulatory submission itself. The FDA
has a dedicated pharmacometrics division inside its Office of Clinical
Pharmacology; the EMA has an equivalent modelling and simulation working
group. Both now use pharmacometric analysis to make approval decisions, not
just to review submissions after the fact.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

The methodological core — nonlinear mixed-effects modelling, NONMEM as the
standard software, exposure-response analysis for small molecules — has
been standard practice since the 1980s. The frontier is expanding faster
here than in any of the other PK/PD subfields:

- **Pharmacometrics for biologics and novel modalities** — monoclonal
  antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, mRNA, gene and cell therapy — is
  roughly a decade behind small-molecule maturity, by most accounts I've
  seen, though I can't point to a rigorous measurement of that gap.
- **Disease progression modelling** — simulating not just drug behaviour
  but how the disease itself evolves, and how treatment changes that
  trajectory — is active and directly tied to reducing how much a trial
  has to rely on real patients to find out what would happen.
- **Model-informed precision dosing** — using real-time patient data plus
  a Bayesian pharmacometric model to individualise a hospital patient's
  dose — is already proven for a handful of drugs (vancomycin,
  tacrolimus) and the open question is scaling it, not whether it works.
  See [therapeutic drug monitoring](/notes/pharma-eng/therapeutic-drug-monitoring)
  for where this connects to the bedside.
- **Quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP)** — bridging pharmacometrics
  with molecular-level systems biology — is the frontier this discipline
  is actively growing into; see
  [systems pharmacology](/notes/pharma-eng/systems-pharmacology).
- **ML-augmented pharmacometrics** — hybrid models combining a mechanistic
  PK/PD structure with machine-learning parameter estimation — is a live
  research area, not a settled toolkit yet.
