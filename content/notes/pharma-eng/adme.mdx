Swallow a tablet and the drug inside it has done nothing yet. Before it can
lower your blood pressure or kill a cancer cell, it has to survive four
separate stages, any one of which can fail.

**Absorption.** Can the drug cross the gut wall and reach the bloodstream at
all? Stomach acid destroys some drugs outright; others simply can't cross
the gut lining, which is why insulin is injected rather than swallowed.

**Distribution.** Once in the blood, where does it actually go? A drug that
never reaches the tumour, or never crosses into the brain, is doing nothing
useful no matter how much of it you give — it might just sit in fat tissue
instead.

**Metabolism.** Mostly in the liver, enzymes — chiefly the CYP450 family —
attack the drug and convert it into other molecules. Sometimes that
deactivates it; sometimes the resulting metabolite is itself active, or
toxic.

**Excretion.** The body has to be able to get rid of what's left, mainly
through the kidneys or bile. A drug that can't be excreted accumulates, and
accumulation is how a safe dose becomes a toxic one.

ADME is the science of measuring and predicting each of these four stages
for a given drug candidate, before it ever reaches a patient.

## Where it sits in development

ADME is a gatekeeper science, front-loaded into discovery and preclinical
work, then carried into early human trials to confirm what the lab work
predicted:

```
Discovery → ADME screens compounds out early
Preclinical → full ADME characterisation
Phase I → human ADME studies confirm or contradict the preclinical model
```

A compound with poor ADME properties — one that never gets absorbed, or
gets destroyed by the liver before it can act — is usually dropped here,
long before anyone spends money on a Phase II trial to find out.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

The four-stage framework itself has been standard since the 1970s, and for a
conventional oral small molecule, ADME is well-characterised biology, not a
research frontier. What's still genuinely open:

- Predicting how a drug will behave **in humans** from animal or cell data
  alone — the inter-species gap is still poorly understood, which is why
  animal studies remain a required step rather than a formality.
- **Blood-brain barrier penetration** is notoriously hard to predict, which
  is a direct bottleneck on CNS drug development — Alzheimer's,
  Parkinson's, psychiatric conditions.
- ADME in populations that don't get studied much — neonates, the elderly,
  patients with liver or kidney disease — is thin.
- The **gut microbiome's** effect on drug metabolism is a real and largely
  unmapped source of the patient-to-patient variability doctors see and
  can't explain.
- ADME for biologics — proteins, mRNA, gene therapies — doesn't behave like
  small-molecule ADME at all, and the field is meaningfully behind on it.

None of those numbers above are mine to put a percentage on with any
confidence; I'd treat any specific "X% of drug failures are ADME-related"
claim you see elsewhere as a rough historical estimate, not a measured
constant — it depends heavily on therapeutic area and on how early ADME
screening happens in that company's pipeline.
