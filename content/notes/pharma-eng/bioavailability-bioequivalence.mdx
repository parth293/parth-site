A brand-name drug goes off patent, and ten generic manufacturers want to
make their own version — same active ingredient, same dose, but different
fillers, coatings, and manufacturing processes. The regulatory question is
simple to state and hard to answer: will these generic versions actually
deliver the same amount of drug, at the same rate, to the same effect?

**Bioavailability** asks how much of a drug reaches the bloodstream, and
how fast. An intravenous injection is 100% bioavailable by definition — it
goes straight into the blood. An oral tablet has to survive the stomach and
cross the gut wall first, so its bioavailability might be 20%, 50%, or 90%,
depending entirely on how it's formulated.

**Bioequivalence** asks whether two formulations of the same drug deliver
statistically equivalent bioavailability — under the regulatory standard
FDA and most other agencies use, the two products' rate and extent of
absorption have to fall within 80–125% of each other.

This is the science that makes the generic drug industry possible at all,
and it also governs every post-approval change a manufacturer makes to an
already-approved drug — a new tablet size, a new coating, a new
manufacturing site — where the company has to demonstrate to a regulator
that the change didn't alter how the drug behaves in the body.

## Where it sits in development

Bioavailability and bioequivalence (BA/BE) work shows up at two distinct
points. Early, it's used inside a single company's own development
programme — comparing formulations, checking for food effects, or
justifying a formulation change mid-trial. Late, it's the primary
requirement for a generic manufacturer's regulatory submission (an ANDA in
the US), and it's required again any time a manufacturing change is big
enough to trigger a fresh bioequivalence study under FDA's SUPAC framework
for post-approval changes.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

For conventional oral small-molecule formulations, this is mature science:
the 80–125% standard has held since the 1970s, and the statistical methods
behind it are well established. The frontier sits almost entirely in
products that don't fit that conventional case:

- **Locally acting drugs** — inhaled, topical, ophthalmic — where blood
  levels don't capture the exposure that actually matters, because the
  drug is meant to act where it's applied, not systemically. This is a
  genuinely unsolved regulatory problem, and it's specifically why generic
  inhalers have taken years longer to reach market than generic tablets.
- **Biosimilars** — demonstrating equivalence for a complex biological
  product, where the generic version is similar but not molecularly
  identical to the original, requires a different, still-developing
  "totality of evidence" framework rather than a single blood-level
  comparison.
- **Highly variable drugs** — ones where the same person, on the same
  drug, on different days, shows more than roughly 30% variation — make
  the standard bioequivalence test statistically unreliable, and the
  reference-scaled alternatives aren't universally accepted across
  regulators yet.
- **In vitro–in vivo correlation (IVIVC)** — predicting how a drug will
  behave in a person from lab dissolution testing alone, without running a
  clinical study — is an active area, particularly for modified-release
  formulations.
