Most drugs are dosed by a simple approximation — a standard amount for a
70kg adult, adjusted a bit for age. For most drugs, in most patients, the
therapeutic window is wide enough that this approximation is safe. For a
handful of drugs, it isn't.

Take tacrolimus, given to transplant patients to stop organ rejection. Too
little, and the immune system attacks the new organ. Too much, and the drug
itself damages the kidneys and raises infection and cancer risk. The gap
between "too little" and "too much" is a concentration range narrow enough
to fit on one graph, and the dose needed to land in that range can vary
ten- to twentyfold between individual patients. You can't guess this dose
safely — you have to measure it.

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is the practice of measuring a drug's
concentration in a patient's blood, usually at a specific time relative to
the last dose, and using that measurement to adjust their individual dose
toward a target range associated with good outcomes. The classic TDM drugs
are tacrolimus and cyclosporine (transplant), vancomycin and the
aminoglycosides (serious infection), phenytoin and carbamazepine
(epilepsy), lithium (bipolar disorder), methotrexate, and digoxin (heart
failure).

The modern version of this — model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) — uses
a Bayesian algorithm that combines a patient's own measured levels with a
population PK model (see [pharmacometrics](/notes/pharma-eng/pharmacometrics))
to predict that specific patient's PK parameters and calculate an
individualised dose in close to real time.

## Where it sits in development

Unlike most of the rest of this pillar, TDM sits almost entirely at the
post-approval, clinical-use end of the pipeline — it's practised in
hospitals, transplant centres, and infectious disease units, not mainly in
a drug-development lab. Earlier-stage work still feeds it: Phase I/II data
identifies whether a drug needs TDM at all (is the therapeutic window
narrow? is patient variability high?), and the target concentration range
that TDM is measured against usually comes out of the same
exposure-response analysis that sets the drug's dosing recommendation.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

Classical trough-level TDM for drugs like digoxin and phenytoin has been
routine clinical practice for more than fifty years. What's moving now:

- **MIPD at scale.** The science of Bayesian dose individualisation is
  proven — the constraint is adoption. Most hospitals still don't use it
  even for vancomycin, where the clinical case for switching from
  trough-only monitoring to full exposure-guided dosing is well
  established.
- **TDM for biologics** — monitoring blood levels of drugs like
  infliximab or adalimumab in inflammatory bowel disease — has real
  evidence behind it for a few specific drugs, but target ranges are still
  poorly defined for most biologics, and clinical uptake is inconsistent.
- **TDM in critically ill patients** is a distinct problem: fluid shifts,
  altered protein binding, and augmented renal clearance mean a model
  built on stable outpatients doesn't transfer cleanly to the ICU.
- **Point-of-care measurement** — getting a drug level back in minutes at
  the bedside instead of hours from a central lab — would make TDM
  practical for drugs where it currently isn't, but the technology isn't
  there yet for most of the classic TDM drugs.
- **Pediatric TDM** target ranges are, more often than not, extrapolated
  from adult data rather than established directly in children.
