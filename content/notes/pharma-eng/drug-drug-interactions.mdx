A 65-year-old with heart disease, diabetes, and mild depression might take
six to eight medications a day, and those drugs don't act in isolation —
they interact, sometimes usefully, sometimes fatally. A drug-drug
interaction (DDI) is what happens when one drug changes how another one
behaves in the body, and it happens through two distinct mechanisms.

**Pharmacokinetic interactions** — one drug changes how another is
absorbed, metabolised, or excreted. The most common route: one drug blocks
or ramps up the liver enzymes (mainly the CYP450 family) that break down
another. Block the enzyme, and the second drug accumulates toward
toxicity; induce it, and the second drug disappears too fast to work.
Grapefruit juice is the standard teaching example — a compound in it
inhibits CYP3A4 and can push certain heart medications to dangerous levels.

**Pharmacodynamic interactions** — two drugs with overlapping effects
amplify or cancel each other, independent of blood concentration. Two
sedatives together sedate more than either alone; an anticoagulant plus an
antiplatelet drug together bleed more than either alone.

DDI science identifies, predicts, and manages these effects — through in
vitro lab assays, computational prediction, clinical studies, and
regulatory risk assessment — so that by the time a drug is approved,
physicians have some idea which combinations are safe.

## Where it sits in development

DDI is both a discovery-stage screen and a late-stage regulatory
requirement. Early in vitro CYP inhibition assays flag compounds with a
severe DDI liability before much money is spent on them; specific clinical
DDI studies, testing the new drug against known "probe" drugs, are required
before approval; and the DDI section of a drug's label — the part stating
known interactions and contraindications — is written from this work and
defended to regulators directly.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

CYP450-mediated small-molecule DDI is mature, mechanistically
well-characterised science built up over roughly forty years, with detailed
regulatory guidance on what data is expected. What's still open:

- **Transporter-mediated interactions** — via proteins like P-gp or
  OATP1B1/1B3 — are meaningfully harder to predict from lab data than
  enzyme-mediated ones, and the regulatory guidance here is still
  evolving.
- **Biologic DDIs** work through a completely different mechanism:
  inflammatory disease changes CYP enzyme expression via cytokines, and a
  monoclonal antibody that neutralises those cytokines can shift the PK of
  a co-administered small molecule substantially. This is an active
  concern as combination biologic regimens become more common.
- **Polypharmacy** — three or more drugs at once — is essentially
  unstudied as a combinatorial problem; almost all DDI science examines
  drugs in pairs, while real patients, especially elderly ones, often
  aren't on just two.
- **Machine learning DDI prediction** from molecular structure alone is an
  active research area with real limitations — reasonably accurate for
  known interaction mechanisms, much less so for novel ones.
- **Pediatric DDI** is thin, because CYP enzyme expression itself changes
  substantially through childhood development.

I'd treat any specific dollar figure or percentage attached to DDI-related
adverse events elsewhere as someone's estimate rather than a fixed number —
it depends heavily on which patient population and which drug classes are
being counted.
