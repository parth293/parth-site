Classical pharmacology finds a receptor, designs a molecule that binds it,
and measures what happens — clean, and often not enough. Biology doesn't
respond to a drug by flipping one switch; it responds through cascades of
interacting proteins, feedback loops, and compensatory pathways that a
single-target experiment can't see. That's a real part of why drugs that
work cleanly in a test tube often fail in patients: the target genuinely
binds, but the network around it responds in ways the isolated experiment
never predicted.

Systems pharmacology takes the network seriously, combining three things at
once: mechanistic biology (the actual signalling pathways and gene
regulatory networks a drug touches), mathematics (ordinary differential
equations and network models that simulate how those pathways behave over
time), and pharmacokinetics (drug concentration, integrated with the
network model so it captures how the biological system evolves over days
or weeks of treatment, not just at one instant). The result is a
computational model of how a disease's biology responds to treatment —
including feedback loops, resistance mechanisms, and combination effects —
built to inform trial design, dose selection, and which patients are
likely to respond at all.

In industry, this discipline is usually called QSP — quantitative systems
pharmacology — and it's the frontier that
[pharmacometrics](/notes/pharma-eng/pharmacometrics) is actively expanding
into as drug development moves further into combination therapies and
biologics.

## Where it sits in development

Systems pharmacology sits earlier than most of the PK/PD work in this
pillar — primarily discovery and translational science, though it's
increasingly reaching into later clinical stages. In discovery, it's used
for network-level target identification: which node in a disease network,
if perturbed, actually shifts the system toward health. In translational
work, it supports animal-to-human dose prediction. In Phase II, it can
guide which measurable biomarker actually predicts clinical benefit, and in
Phase III, which patient subpopulation is likeliest to respond, based on
their baseline biology. Regulatory use is still early — the FDA has been
exploring systems-pharmacology models specifically in oncology, but this
isn't yet a routine part of a submission the way a PopPK report is.

## What's actually settled, and what isn't

This is younger, as a discipline, than any other subfield in this pillar.
The underlying mathematical frameworks and specific well-characterised
signalling pathways (MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and a handful of others) are
established; a genuinely comprehensive, validated model of a whole disease
network isn't. What's open:

- **Predicting drug combination effects from first principles.** Why two
  drugs that each work alone sometimes fail, or become toxic, together is
  something a systems model should in principle be able to predict — in
  practice, oncology combination regimens are still selected largely by
  empirical trial and error.
- **Modelling acquired resistance** — how cancer cells, bacteria, or
  viruses evolve under drug pressure — is only partially captured by
  current models.
- **Multi-scale integration**, connecting molecular-level biology through
  cellular and tissue behaviour to a clinical outcome in one validated
  framework, doesn't really exist yet for any disease. Some people call
  the eventual patient-specific version of this a "digital twin"; it's an
  aspiration more than a working method right now.
- **Immuno-oncology specifically** is a hard open problem: the tumour
  microenvironment is an ecosystem of cancer cells, immune cells, stromal
  cells, and vasculature, and only a minority of patients respond to
  checkpoint inhibitors — a systems model that reliably predicted who
  would respond, and why, would be a genuinely large result.
