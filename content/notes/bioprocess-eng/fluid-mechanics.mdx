## What is a fluid?

A **fluid** is a substance that flows and takes the shape of the container it
is in. Liquids and gases are both fluids — "fluid" is the broader category,
not a synonym for "liquid."

- Liquids are fluids.
- Gases are fluids.

## Classification of fluids

### Ideal fluids vs. real (non-ideal) fluids

- **Ideal fluids**: inviscid (zero viscosity), incompressible — a
  hypothetical simplification; no fluid is actually ideal.
- **Real (non-ideal) fluids**: viscous — this is the defining property, not
  compressibility. Compressibility varies independently: most liquids are
  real but still treated as incompressible, while gases are real and
  compressible.

### Newtonian vs. non-Newtonian fluids

Another way to classify fluids, based on their flow behavior.

## Rheology

**Rheology** is the study of the flow of a fluid.

A fluid is a substance that undergoes deformation when subjected to shearing
stress.

### Shear stress

**Shear stress** is the tangential force applied per unit area.

$$
\tau = \frac{F}{A}
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$) or newtons per square metre
  ($\text{N/m}^2$)
- $F$ — tangential force, in newtons ($\text{N}$)
- $A$ — area, in square metres ($\text{m}^2$)

![Two parallel plates with fluid between them: force F applied over area A produces shear stress τ = F/A](/notes/bioprocess-eng/shear-stress-diagram.png)

### Shear rate

Shear rate is the strain that occurs within a fluid, representing the change
in velocity of adjacent fluid layers over a distance.

A fluid can be thought of as stacked layers, each moving at a different
velocity. The topmost layer has no force acting on it from above, so it moves
freely. Each layer below experiences a force from the layer above it, which
slows it down further — down to the bottom-most layer, at the floor, which is
nearly static, at zero velocity.

![Fluid layers under an applied force, each moving at a different velocity: the topmost layer moves at v1, velocity decreases going down through the stack, and the bottom layer is at zero velocity — the velocity gradient over height h](/notes/bioprocess-eng/shear-rate-velocity-profile.png)

$$
\dot{\gamma} = \frac{v}{h}
$$

- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate (velocity gradient), in reciprocal seconds
  ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $v$ — velocity of the topmost fluid layer, in metres per second ($\text{m/s}$)
- $h$ — height of the fluid layer, in metres ($\text{m}$)

### Viscosity

**Viscosity** is the internal resistance of a fluid to continuous shear
deformation, or to the rate of strain, under an applied shear stress.

### Kinematic viscosity

**Kinematic viscosity** is the ratio of a fluid's dynamic viscosity to its
density.

$$
\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}
$$

- $\nu$ — kinematic viscosity, in square metres per second ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
- $\mu$ — dynamic viscosity, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $\rho$ — density of the fluid, in kilograms per cubic metre ($\text{kg/m}^3$)

Dividing out density leaves a purely kinematic quantity (length and time only,
no mass or force) — this is what governs how a velocity disturbance diffuses
through a fluid under viscous and inertial forces alone, independent of how
heavy the fluid is. This is why the Reynolds number can equivalently be
written $Re = vd/\nu$, using kinematic rather than dynamic viscosity.

#### Worked example: kinematic viscosity of water

**Question.** Water at room temperature has a dynamic viscosity of
$1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$ and a density of
$1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$. What is its kinematic viscosity?

**Solution.**

1. Start from $\nu = \mu / \rho$.
2. Substitute:
   $\nu = \dfrac{1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}}{1000\ \text{kg/m}^3}$.
3. $1\ \text{Pa} \cdot \text{s} = 1\ \text{kg/(m} \cdot \text{s)}$, so the
   units reduce to $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$.
4. $\nu = 1 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

**Answer:** $\nu \approx 1 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

### Power law equation (Ostwald–de Waele model)

For non-Newtonian fluids only.

$$
\tau = K \dot{\gamma}^n
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $K$ — consistency index (generalized viscosity), in $\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}^n$
  (or $\text{N} \cdot \text{s}^n / \text{m}^2$): $K \uparrow \rightarrow$
  viscosity $\uparrow$; $K \downarrow \rightarrow$ viscosity $\downarrow$
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $n$ — flow behaviour index (dimensionless)

| $n$ | Fluid type | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $n = 1$ | Newtonian | — |
| $n > 1$ | Dilatant (shear-thickening) | $\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu \uparrow$ |
| $n < 1$ | Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning) | $\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu \downarrow$ — e.g. ketchup, which needs to be shaken |

#### Apparent viscosity of a power-law fluid

A non-Newtonian fluid has no single constant viscosity — it changes with
shear rate. Restating the power law as an **apparent viscosity**
($\mu_a = \tau / \dot\gamma$) puts a shear-rate-dependent number in the same
slot a constant $\mu$ would occupy, so it can be substituted directly into
equations built for Newtonian fluids (e.g. the Reynolds number) at whatever
shear rate the flow is actually running at.

$$
\mu_a = K \dot{\gamma}^{\,n-1}
$$

- $\mu_a$ — apparent viscosity, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $K$ — consistency index, in $\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}^n$
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $n$ — flow behaviour index (dimensionless)

#### Worked example: apparent viscosity change with shear rate

**Question.** For a power law fluid with flow behaviour index $n = 0.6$, if
the shear rate is increased by a factor of $32$, by what factor does the
apparent viscosity change?

**Solution.**

1. Start from $\mu_a = K \dot{\gamma}^{\,n-1}$.
2. $K$ is fixed for a given fluid, so it cancels in a ratio:
   $\dfrac{\mu_{a2}}{\mu_{a1}} = \left(\dfrac{\dot\gamma_2}{\dot\gamma_1}\right)^{n-1}$.
3. Substitute $\dot\gamma_2/\dot\gamma_1 = 32$ and $n - 1 = 0.6 - 1 = -0.4$.
4. $32^{-0.4} = (2^5)^{-0.4} = 2^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$.
5. Sanity check: $n < 1$ is pseudoplastic (shear-thinning), so apparent
   viscosity must fall as shear rate rises — a ratio below $1$ is consistent.

**Answer:** Apparent viscosity decreases by a factor of $4$.

#### Worked example: reading $n$ off a log-log plot

**Question.** In a log-log plot of shear stress ($\tau$) versus shear rate
($\dot{\gamma}$), the slope of the line equals $n$, the flow behaviour index.
For a shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) fluid, what is the value of $n$?

**Solution.**

1. Start from the power law equation: $\tau = K \dot{\gamma}^n$.
2. Take the log of both sides:
   $\log \tau = \log K + n \log \dot{\gamma}$.
3. This is a straight line in $\log \tau$ vs. $\log \dot{\gamma}$ space, with
   slope $n$ and $y$-intercept $\log K$ — so the slope read off a log-log
   plot of $\tau$ against $\dot{\gamma}$ is exactly $n$.
4. A shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) fluid is defined, per the table above, by
   $n < 1$: apparent viscosity falls as shear stress rises.

**Answer:** $n < 1$ — the log-log line is shallower than the $n = 1$
Newtonian line (still positive slope, just less steep).

#### Newton's law of viscosity

When $n = 1$, $K$ is replaced by $\mu$ (mu), the dynamic viscosity:

$$
\tau = \mu \dot{\gamma} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\tau}{\dot{\gamma}} = \mu
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $\mu$ — dynamic viscosity, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)

This is **Newton's law of viscosity**. Fluids that follow it are **Newtonian
fluids**; fluids that don't are **non-Newtonian fluids**. Physical
significance: for a Newtonian fluid, viscosity is constant — it is not
affected by shear stress.

![Shear stress τ plotted against shear rate for a Newtonian fluid: a straight line through the origin, where the slope is the viscosity (n = 1). Examples: water, S. cerevisiae broth, gases](/notes/bioprocess-eng/newtonian-fluid-curve.png)

Examples of Newtonian fluids: water, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (S.
cerevisiae) fermentation broth, and most gases.

> *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* exhibits Newtonian behaviour at low biomass
> concentration; at high cell density (greater than 50 g/L dry cell weight),
> it becomes pseudoplastic — a fluid with dual nature.

### Types of non-Newtonian fluids

![Shear stress τ plotted against shear rate (du/dy) for every fluid type on one chart: ideal Bingham plastic (straight line from a positive yield stress), plastic fluid / Casson fluid (curving from the same yield stress), pseudoplastic (n less than 1, curving up steeply then flattening), Newtonian (n equals 1, a straight line through the origin), and dilatant (n greater than 1, curving up slowly then steeply) — bounded by the ideal solid (vertical axis) and ideal fluid (horizontal axis) limits](/notes/bioprocess-eng/fluid-classification-curves.png)

#### Bingham plastics

A Bingham plastic is a solid below the yield stress, and a viscous Newtonian
fluid above the yield stress.

Bingham plastics require a threshold shear stress — $\tau_0$ or $\tau_y$
(yield stress) — before any shear rate is generated at all.

![Shear stress τ plotted against shear rate for a Bingham plastic: flat at zero shear rate until the yield stress τ0 (or τy) is crossed, then rising linearly](/notes/bioprocess-eng/bingham-plastic-curve.png)

$$
\tau = \tau_y + \mu_p \dot{\gamma}
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $\tau_y$ — yield stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $\mu_p$ — plastic viscosity (also called apparent viscosity), in
  pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)

Examples: tomato ketchup.

#### Pseudoplastics

$n < 1$. As shear stress increases, apparent viscosity decreases:
$\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu_a \downarrow$.

Examples: adhesives (e.g. Fevicol), polymer solutions, rubber solutions,
xanthan gum, detergent slurry, mycelial fermentation broth, and human blood.

#### Dilatant

$n > 1$. As shear stress increases, viscosity also increases:
$\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu_a \uparrow$.

Examples: corn starch, cement slurry.

#### Casson plastics

Casson fluids show dual nature:

- Below the threshold (yield) stress $\tau_0$: as $\tau$ increases, the fluid
  behaves like a Bingham plastic.
- Above $\tau_0$: as $\tau$ increases further, apparent viscosity decreases —
  $\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu_a \downarrow$.

$$
\sqrt{\tau} = \sqrt{\tau_0} + \sqrt{K_c \dot{\gamma}}
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $\tau_0$ — Casson yield stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $K_c$ — Casson viscosity coefficient, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)

Examples: printing ink, tomato ketchup, orange juice, human blood, chocolate.

#### Herschel–Bulkley model

The **Herschel–Bulkley model** generalizes Bingham plastics and power law
fluids into one equation: a yield stress plus a power law response above it.

$$
\tau = \tau_y + K \dot{\gamma}^n
$$

- $\tau$ — shear stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $\tau_y$ — yield stress, in pascals ($\text{Pa}$)
- $K$ — consistency index, in $\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}^n$
- $\dot{\gamma}$ — shear rate, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $n$ — flow behaviour index (dimensionless)

Setting $\tau_y = 0$ recovers the power law equation; setting $n = 1$
recovers the Bingham plastic equation.

#### Comparing the fluid types

| Fluid type | $n$ | Yield stress | Relation | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Newtonian | $n = 1$ | No | $\tau = \mu \dot{\gamma}$ | Water, *S. cerevisiae* broth, most gases |
| Bingham plastic | — | Yes ($\tau_y$) | $\tau = \tau_y + \mu_p \dot{\gamma}$ | Tomato ketchup |
| Pseudoplastic | $n < 1$ | No | $\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu_a \downarrow$ | Adhesives (Fevicol), polymer solutions, rubber solutions, xanthan gum, detergent slurry, mycelial fermentation broth, human blood |
| Dilatant | $n > 1$ | No | $\tau \uparrow \rightarrow \mu_a \uparrow$ | Corn starch, cement slurry |
| Casson plastic | — | Yes ($\tau_0$), dual nature | Bingham-like below $\tau_0$; pseudoplastic-like above | Printing ink, tomato ketchup, orange juice, human blood, chocolate |

## Types of fluid flow

Flow is classified into two regimes based on the pattern of fluid motion:
**laminar** and **turbulent**.

**Laminar flow** (streamlined flow) moves in parallel layers, with streamlines
that never cross. It is slow-moving and not recommended for mixing.

**Turbulent flow** is chaotic, irregular motion in which **eddies** form. It
is recommended for mixing.

![Two pipes compared: laminar flow shows straight, parallel arrows moving uniformly along the pipe; turbulent flow shows swirling, circular eddies scattered throughout the pipe](/notes/bioprocess-eng/laminar-turbulent-flow-pipe.png)

| | Laminar flow | Turbulent flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pattern of motion | Parallel layers; streamlines do not cross | Chaotic, irregular |
| Velocity at a point | Steady with respect to time (non-chaotic) | Magnitude and direction both change; large, abrupt changes |
| Eddies | None | Formed |
| Speed | Slow-moving | — |
| Mixing | Not recommended | Recommended |

### Common confusion: vortex vs. turbulent flow

Continuous rotation in a liquid produces a **vortex**. A vortex is often
mistaken for turbulent flow, but it is not turbulent — it does not help
mixing. Instead, it allows solids to settle out and precipitate toward the
bottom.

## Reynolds number

The **Reynolds number** is the parameter that indicates whether a fluid is in
laminar or turbulent flow. It represents the ratio of inertial forces to
viscous forces in the fluid, and is dimensionless (unitless).

### Reynolds number for stirred tank

{/* importance: very-important */}

$$
Re = \frac{d_i^2 N_i \rho}{\mu}
$$

- $Re$ — Reynolds number (ratio of inertial to viscous forces), dimensionless
- $d_i$ — diameter of the impeller, in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $N_i$ — frequency of the impeller, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $\rho$ — density of the fluid, in kilograms per cubic metre ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
- $\mu$ — viscosity, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $Re < 10$ — laminar
- $10 \le Re \le 10{,}000$ — transitional
- $Re > 10{,}000$ — turbulent

### Reynolds number for straight pipe

$$
Re = \frac{d v \rho}{\mu}
$$

- $Re$ — Reynolds number (ratio of inertial to viscous forces), dimensionless
- $d$ — diameter of the pipe, in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $v$ — average velocity of the fluid, in metres per second ($\text{m/s}$)
- $\rho$ — density of the fluid, in kilograms per cubic metre ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
- $\mu$ — viscosity, in pascal-seconds ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $Re < 2{,}100$ — laminar
- $Re > 4{,}000$ — turbulent

### Flow regime by Reynolds number

| System | Laminar | Transitional | Turbulent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stirred tank | $Re < 10$ | $10 \le Re \le 10{,}000$ | $Re > 10{,}000$ |
| Straight pipe | $Re < 2{,}100$ | — | $Re > 4{,}000$ |

### Converting impeller speed: RPM to reciprocal seconds

Impeller speed is often given in RPM (revolutions per minute), but $N_i$ in
the equations above is in reciprocal seconds. Convert by dividing by 60:

$$
N_i \, [\text{s}^{-1}] = \frac{N_i \, [\text{RPM}]}{60}
$$

## Froude number

The **Froude number** is the parameter that represents the ratio of inertial
forces to gravitational (buoyancy) forces.

At high impeller speed, inertial forces dominate gravity enough to distort
the liquid's free surface — pulling it into a vortex and drawing in air — so
$Fr$ is tracked alongside $Re$ during scale-up to catch that surface effect.

### Froude number for stirred tank

$$
Fr = \frac{N_i^2 d_i}{g}
$$

- $Fr$ — Froude number (ratio of inertial to gravitational/buoyancy forces), dimensionless
- $N_i$ — frequency of the impeller, in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $d_i$ — diameter of the impeller, in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $g$ — acceleration due to gravity, $9.8 \ \text{m/s}^2$

## Key points

- **Ideal fluids** are inviscid and incompressible (a hypothetical
  simplification); **real fluids** are viscous — compressibility is a
  separate, independent property.
- **Newton's law of viscosity** ($\tau = \mu\dot\gamma$) only holds for
  Newtonian fluids, where $\mu$ is constant; non-Newtonian fluids need an
  **apparent viscosity** ($\mu_a = K\dot\gamma^{\,n-1}$) that varies with
  shear rate instead.
- Flow behaviour index $n$ classifies non-Newtonian fluids: $n < 1$
  pseudoplastic (shear-thinning), $n > 1$ dilatant (shear-thickening).
- **Bingham plastics** need a yield stress $\tau_y$ before they flow at all;
  **Casson** and **Herschel–Bulkley** fluids combine a yield stress with
  shear-thinning/power-law behaviour above it.
- Reynolds number thresholds differ by system: stirred tank is laminar below
  $Re = 10$ and turbulent above $Re = 10{,}000$; straight pipe is laminar
  below $Re = 2{,}100$ and turbulent above $Re = 4{,}000$ — don't cross-apply
  the two.
- Turbulent flow (with eddies) is needed for mixing; laminar flow is not, and
  neither is a vortex — a vortex is easy to mistake for turbulence but
  actually promotes settling, not mixing.
