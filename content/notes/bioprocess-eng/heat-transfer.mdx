## Modes of heat transfer

There are three modes of heat transfer: **conduction**, **convection**, and
**radiation**. Radiation is out of syllabus for this section.

## Conduction

**Conduction** is heat transfer that occurs through the transfer of
vibrational energy between molecules, or through the movement of free
electrons. No mobility or movement of matter is involved in conduction.

![Hot fluid on the left, cold fluid on the right, separated by a metal wall in the middle; an arrow through the wall marks the direction of heat flow, and the wall's thickness is labelled B](/notes/bioprocess-eng/conduction-hot-cold-fluid-metal-wall.png)

### Rate of heat transfer by conduction

$$
\hat{Q} = -kA\frac{dT}{dy} = -kA\frac{T_2 - T_1}{y}
$$

- $\hat{Q}$ — rate of heat transfer (thermal energy crossing area $A$ per
  unit time), in watts ($\text{W}$)
- $k$ — thermal conductivity of the wall, in watts per metre-kelvin
  ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$)
- $A$ — area perpendicular to heat transfer, in square metres ($\text{m}^2$)
- $dT/dy$ — temperature gradient, in kelvin per metre ($\text{K/m}$)
- $T_2, T_1$ — temperatures either side of the wall, in kelvin ($\text{K}$)
- $y$ — distance component of thickness, in metres ($\text{m}$)

The first form is differential — it holds at a point, for the local
gradient $dT/dy$. Integrating it across a wall of constant thickness and
conductivity gives the second, finite-thickness form, which swaps the
gradient for the finite difference $(T_2 - T_1)/y$ across the whole wall.

The negative sign shows that heat is transferred from the hot side to the
cold side. In the finite-thickness form, the sign in front of $kA$ depends
on which temperature is subtracted first:

- Subtracting $T_{cold} - T_{hot}$: use a negative sign.
- Subtracting $T_{hot} - T_{cold}$: use a positive sign.

Either way, the final value of $\hat{Q}$ comes out positive.

## Convection

**Convection** is heat transfer that occurs through the movement of
molecules.

![Diagram of heat moving from a hot bulk fluid (Th) to a cold bulk fluid (Tc) through a metal wall: convection carries heat from Th to the hot-side wall temperature (Thw), conduction carries it through the wall from Thw to the cold-side wall temperature (Tcw), and convection carries it from Tcw to Tc](/notes/bioprocess-eng/convection-temperature-profile-composite-wall.png)

Heat crossing from a hot bulk fluid to a cold bulk fluid through a wall moves
in three steps:

1. **Convection** — hot bulk fluid ($T_h$) to the hot-side wall temperature
   ($T_{hw}$).
2. **Conduction** — through the wall, from $T_{hw}$ to the cold-side wall
   temperature ($T_{cw}$).
3. **Convection** — $T_{cw}$ to the cold bulk fluid ($T_c$).

Two temperature gradients drive the two convective steps:

1. $T_h - T_{hw}$ — convection in the hot fluid.
2. $T_{cw} - T_c$ — convection in the cold fluid.

### Rate of heat transfer by convection

$$
\hat{Q} = hA \Delta T
$$

- $\hat{Q}$ — rate of heat transfer (thermal energy crossing area $A$ per
  unit time), in watts ($\text{W}$)
- $h$ — individual (film) heat transfer coefficient — how readily heat
  crosses between the bulk fluid and the wall by convection, in watts per
  square metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K})$)
- $A$ — area, in square metres ($\text{m}^2$)
- $\Delta T$ — temperature gradient, in kelvin ($\text{K}$)

$h$ depends on:

| Factor | Effect on $h$ |
| --- | --- |
| Higher flow velocity / agitation (more turbulence) | Increases $h$ — thins the boundary film |
| Lower viscosity (e.g. higher temperature) | Increases $h$ — less resistance to flow near the wall |
| Thicker fluid film / boundary layer | Decreases $h$ — more stagnant layer to conduct across |
| Higher thermal conductivity of the fluid | Increases $h$ |
| Fouling, biofilm, or cake buildup at the wall | Decreases $h$ — adds a stagnant insulating layer (captured separately as $R_f$) |

#### Worked example: which changes increase h in a stirred-tank bioreactor

**Question.** Which of the following operational modifications will increase
the inside convective heat transfer coefficient ($h$) in a stirred tank
bioreactor? (a) Increasing the impeller agitation speed (b) Increasing the
broth temperature (which lowers viscosity) (c) Accumulation of a dense
mycelial wall cake (d) Decreasing the broth flow velocity past the heat
exchange surface.

**Solution.**

1. **(a) Increasing agitation speed** — raises turbulence and flow velocity
   at the wall, thinning the boundary film. Increases $h$.
2. **(b) Increasing broth temperature (lower viscosity)** — less resistance
   to flow near the wall, effectively raising the Reynolds number. Increases
   $h$.
3. **(c) Mycelial wall cake** — a solid, low-conductivity layer stuck to the
   wall acts as fouling: it adds resistance and dampens turbulence at the
   surface. Decreases $h$.
4. **(d) Decreasing flow velocity** — less turbulence, thicker boundary
   film. Decreases $h$.

**Answer:** (a) and (b) increase $h$; (c) and (d) decrease it.

## Nusselt number

The **Nusselt number** is the ratio between convective heat transfer and
conductive heat transfer.

$$
Nu = \frac{hD}{k_{fb}}
$$

- $Nu$ — Nusselt number (ratio of convective to conductive heat transfer), dimensionless
- $h$ — individual heat transfer coefficient, in watts per square
  metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K})$)
- $D$ — diameter of the pipe or tank, or characteristic length of the pipe,
  in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $k_{fb}$ — thermal conductivity of the bulk fluid, in watts per
  metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$)

## Prandtl number

The **Prandtl number** is the ratio of momentum diffusivity (kinematic
viscosity, $\nu = \mu/\rho$) to thermal diffusivity ($\alpha = k/(\rho C_p)$).
Dividing both by $\rho$ cancels it out, leaving $Pr = C_p \mu_b / k_{fb}$ —
the form actually used below.

$$
Pr = \frac{C_p \mu_b}{k_{fb}}
$$

- $Pr$ — Prandtl number (ratio of molecular momentum to thermal diffusivity), dimensionless
- $C_p$ — heat capacity, in joules per kilogram-kelvin
  ($\text{J}/(\text{kg} \cdot \text{K})$)
- $\mu_b$ — viscosity of the bulk fluid, in pascal-seconds
  ($\text{Pa} \cdot \text{s}$)
- $k_{fb}$ — thermal conductivity of the bulk fluid, in watts per
  metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$)

> In these problems the chain typically runs backwards from what you want:
> to get $\hat{Q}$ you need $h$; to get $h$ you need the Nusselt number; to
> get the Nusselt number you need $k_{fb}$; and for $k_{fb}$ you use $Pr$.

## Conductive resistance of a wall

**Conductive (thermal) resistance** is how much a layer of material resists
heat flow, per unit area — thicker or less-conductive material resists
more, the same logic as electrical resistance.

$$
R_{cond} = \frac{x}{k}
$$

- $R_{cond}$ — conductive thermal resistance per unit area, in square
  metre-kelvin per watt ($\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K}/\text{W}$)
- $x$ — thickness of the layer, in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $k$ — thermal conductivity of the layer's material, in watts per
  metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$)

For a **composite wall** (several layers stacked in the direction of heat
flow, e.g. a metal shell plus insulation), each layer's resistance adds,
the same as resistors in series:

$$
R_{total} = \sum_i \frac{x_i}{k_i}
$$

- $R_{total}$ — total conductive resistance of the composite wall per unit
  area, in square metre-kelvin per watt ($\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K}/\text{W}$)
- $x_i, k_i$ — thickness and thermal conductivity of layer $i$, in metres
  ($\text{m}$) and watts per metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot
  \text{K})$) respectively

If convective film resistances aren't negligible, they add into the same
sum as $1/h$ terms — see the overall-$U$ equation below, which is exactly
this same series-resistance idea with the film coefficients included.

#### Worked example: composite bioreactor wall

**Question.** A composite bioreactor wall has an inner stainless steel
sheet ($x = 5\ \text{mm}$, $k = 15\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{K})$) and
outer insulation ($x = 25\ \text{mm}$, $k = 0.05\ \text{W}/(\text{m}\cdot
\text{K})$). Convective resistances are negligible. Find the total
conductive resistance per unit area.

**Solution.**

1. Steel: $R_1 = 0.005/15 = 0.000333\ (\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K})/\text{W}$.
2. Insulation: $R_2 = 0.025/0.05 = 0.500\ (\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K})/\text{W}$.
3. $R_{total} = R_1 + R_2 = 0.500\ (\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K})/\text{W}$ (to
   3 d.p.) — the insulation dominates since its conductivity is $300\times$
   lower than the steel's.

**Answer:** $R_{total} \approx 0.500\ (\text{m}^2\cdot\text{K})/\text{W}$.

## Overall heat transfer coefficient

The chain above requires measuring $\Delta T$ at each interface inside the
bioprocess reactor, which is very difficult to measure. A newer methodology
avoids this, using the **overall heat transfer coefficient** ($U$), in watts
per square metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K})$).

### Rate of heat transfer using U

$$
\hat{Q} = UA \Delta T_{LMTD}
$$

- $\hat{Q}$ — rate of heat transfer (thermal energy crossing area $A$ per
  unit time), in watts ($\text{W}$)
- $U$ — overall heat transfer coefficient (lumps both film coefficients and
  the wall's own conduction resistance into one number, avoiding the need
  to measure $\Delta T$ at each internal interface), in watts per square
  metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K})$)
- $A$ — area, in square metres ($\text{m}^2$)
- $\Delta T_{LMTD}$ — logarithmic mean temperature difference, in kelvin
  ($\text{K}$)

### Overall heat transfer coefficient from film coefficients

$$
\frac{1}{U} = \frac{1}{h_1} + \frac{x}{k} + \frac{1}{h_2} + R_f
$$

- $U$ — overall heat transfer coefficient (lumps both film coefficients and
  the wall's own conduction resistance into one number), in watts per
  square metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K})$)
- $h_1, h_2$ — individual film heat transfer coefficients on either side of
  the wall, in watts per square metre-kelvin ($\text{W}/(\text{m}^2 \cdot
  \text{K})$)
- $x$ — thickness of the wall, in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $k$ — thermal conductivity of the wall, in watts per metre-kelvin
  ($\text{W}/(\text{m} \cdot \text{K})$)
- $R_f$ — fouling resistance at a film surface (e.g. microbial biofilm or
  scale buildup), in square metre-kelvin per watt ($\text{m}^2 \cdot
  \text{K}/\text{W}$)

All four terms are resistances in series, so $1/U$ is their sum — the same
logic as resistors in series. Unlike $h_1$, $h_2$, and $k$, $R_f$ is not a
fixed property: it starts near zero on a clean surface and grows over the
run as biofilm, protein deposits, or scale build up, steadily lowering $U$
for the same equipment. **Clean-in-place (CIP)** cycles reset $R_f$ back
toward zero; equipment is often sized against an assumed fouled $U$ so it
still performs adequately near the end of a cleaning cycle.

### Logarithmic mean temperature difference (LMTD)

$$
\Delta T_{LMTD} = \frac{\Delta T_2 - \Delta T_1}{\ln(\Delta T_2 / \Delta T_1)}
$$

- $\Delta T_{LMTD}$ — logarithmic mean temperature difference (single
  equivalent driving-force temperature for the whole exchanger, replacing
  the $\Delta T$ that actually varies along its length), in kelvin
  ($\text{K}$)
- $\Delta T_2, \Delta T_1$ — temperature differences at the two ends of the
  heat exchanger, in kelvin ($\text{K}$)

**Special case — equal terminal differences.** When $\Delta T_1 = \Delta
T_2$, the formula above is $0/0$ (indeterminate). Taking the limit as
$\Delta T_2 \to \Delta T_1$ shows the log mean converges to the arithmetic
mean, so $\Delta T_{LMTD} = \Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$ in that case.

### Flow arrangement: parallel vs. counter-current

$\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ depend on how the hot and cold fluids are
routed through the exchanger:

- **Parallel flow (co-current)**: both fluids enter at the same end and
  exit at the same end.
- **Counter-current flow**: the hot fluid's inlet pairs with the cold
  fluid's outlet, and the hot fluid's outlet pairs with the cold fluid's
  inlet.

#### Worked example: LMTD for parallel vs. counter-current flow

**Question.** A hot fluid enters a heat exchanger at $120\,^\circ\text{C}$
and leaves at $80\,^\circ\text{C}$. A cold fluid enters at
$20\,^\circ\text{C}$ and leaves at $50\,^\circ\text{C}$. Find the LMTD for
(a) parallel flow and (b) counter-current flow.

**Solution.**

1. **Parallel flow (co-current)** — both fluids enter at the same end, so
   the inlet ends pair together and the outlet ends pair together:

   ![Temperature profile along the exchanger for parallel flow: the hot fluid falls from 120°C at x=0 to 80°C at x=1, the cold fluid rises from 20°C at x=0 to 50°C at x=1, both flowing left to right](/notes/bioprocess-eng/parallel-flow-temperature-profile.png)

   - $\Delta T_1 = 120 - 20 = 100\,^\circ\text{C}$ (both inlets)
   - $\Delta T_2 = 80 - 50 = 30\,^\circ\text{C}$ (both outlets)
   - $\Delta T_{LMTD} = \dfrac{\Delta T_2 - \Delta T_1}{\ln(\Delta T_2 /
     \Delta T_1)} = \dfrac{30 - 100}{\ln(30/100)} = \dfrac{-70}{-1.204}
     \approx 58.14\,^\circ\text{C}$
2. **Counter-current flow** — the hot inlet pairs with the cold outlet, and
   the hot outlet pairs with the cold inlet:

   ![Temperature profile along the exchanger for counter-current flow: the hot fluid falls from 120°C at x=0 to 80°C at x=1 flowing left to right, the cold fluid falls from 50°C at x=0 to 20°C at x=1 flowing right to left](/notes/bioprocess-eng/counter-current-flow-temperature-profile.png)

   - $\Delta T_1 = 120 - 50 = 70\,^\circ\text{C}$ (hot inlet, cold outlet)
   - $\Delta T_2 = 80 - 20 = 60\,^\circ\text{C}$ (hot outlet, cold inlet)
   - $\Delta T_{LMTD} = \dfrac{60 - 70}{\ln(60/70)} = \dfrac{-10}{-0.1542}
     \approx 64.87\,^\circ\text{C}$

**Answer:** LMTD $\approx 58.14\,^\circ\text{C}$ for parallel flow, and
$\approx 64.87\,^\circ\text{C}$ for counter-current flow — the
counter-current arrangement gives the larger LMTD for the same four
terminal temperatures.

#### Worked example: LMTD when the terminal differences are equal

**Question.** A counter-current heat exchanger cools hot oil from
$120\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $80\,^\circ\text{C}$ using cooling water that heats
from $30\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $70\,^\circ\text{C}$. Find the LMTD.

**Solution.**

1. Counter-current flow: the hot inlet pairs with the cold outlet, and the
   hot outlet pairs with the cold inlet.
   - $\Delta T_1 = 120 - 70 = 50\,^\circ\text{C}$ (hot inlet, cold outlet)
   - $\Delta T_2 = 80 - 30 = 50\,^\circ\text{C}$ (hot outlet, cold inlet)
2. $\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$, so $\dfrac{\Delta T_2 - \Delta T_1}{\ln(\Delta
   T_2/\Delta T_1)}$ is $0/0$. By the equal-terminal-differences limit,
   $\Delta T_{LMTD} = \Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$.

**Answer:** LMTD $= 50.0\,^\circ\text{C}$.

#### Why counter-current is never worse than parallel flow

For fixed terminal temperatures (same hot-in/hot-out/cold-in/cold-out), let
$\Delta H = T_{h,in} - T_{h,out}$ (how much the hot fluid cools) and
$\Delta C = T_{c,out} - T_{c,in}$ (how much the cold fluid warms) — both
positive in any real exchanger.

- **The sum $\Delta T_1 + \Delta T_2$ is identical either way** — it works
  out to $T_{h,in}+T_{h,out}-T_{c,in}-T_{c,out}$ for both arrangements. In
  the worked example above: parallel gives $100+30=130$, counter-current
  gives $70+60=130$ — the same.
- **The spread differs.** For parallel flow, $\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2 =
  \Delta H + \Delta C$. For counter-current, $\Delta T_1 - \Delta T_2 =
  \Delta H - \Delta C$. Since $\Delta H, \Delta C > 0$, $|\Delta H + \Delta
  C| > |\Delta H - \Delta C|$ always — parallel flow's two $\Delta T$s are
  always farther apart than counter-current's, given the same sum.

The logarithmic mean of two positive numbers with a fixed sum is largest
when the numbers are equal — it sits between the geometric and arithmetic
mean, and moves toward the arithmetic mean as the numbers converge. Since
counter-current always keeps $\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ closer together
than parallel flow does, its LMTD is always $\geq$ the parallel LMTD.

The one case they tie: $\Delta H = 0$ or $\Delta C = 0$ — one side doesn't
actually change temperature, as with a condensing vapor or boiling liquid
at constant temperature. Then $\Delta T_1 = \Delta T_2$ for both
arrangements and there is no difference between them. Outside that edge
case, counter-current strictly wins.

## Heat generation and removal in bioprocesses

Aerobic fermentations generate **metabolic heat** as respiration proceeds,
on top of any mechanical heat added by agitation — cooling equipment has to
remove both, and metabolic heat is what usually dominates at scale.

### Metabolic heat from oxygen consumption (Cooney's correlation)

Heat release tracks oxygen consumption because both come from the same
respiratory electron-transport reactions — the more $O_2$ the culture
consumes, the more heat it releases.

$$
Q_{metabolic} = \Delta H_{O_2} \times n_{O_2}
$$

- $Q_{metabolic}$ — metabolic heat released by aerobic respiration, in
  kilojoules ($\text{kJ}$)
- $\Delta H_{O_2}$ — heat released per mole of oxygen consumed
  (**Cooney's correlation**), approximately $460\,\text{kJ/mol}$
  ($\approx 14\,\text{kJ/g}$ $O_2$, since $460/32 \approx 14.4$)
- $n_{O_2}$ — moles of oxygen consumed by the culture ($\text{mol}$)

### Scale-up heat transfer challenge

$$
\frac{A}{V} \propto \frac{D^2}{D^3} = \frac{1}{D}
$$

- $A$ — cooling surface area available (jacket or coil), in square metres
  ($\text{m}^2$)
- $V$ — reactor working volume, in cubic metres ($\text{m}^3$)
- $D$ — characteristic vessel diameter, in metres ($\text{m}$)

Reactor volume scales with $D^3$ but the wall area available for cooling
only scales with $D^2$, so the specific cooling area $A/V$ falls as $1/D$.
Metabolic heat generation scales roughly with volume (more broth, more
respiring cells), so larger fermenters generate heat faster than their
jacket area can remove it — the classic bioreactor scale-up cooling
bottleneck.

### Jacket vs. internal cooling coil

| Feature | External jacket | Internal cooling coil |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cooling area | Limited to the vessel wall | Extra area, added independently of vessel wall size |
| Shear on cells | Low — no extra internal surfaces | Higher — coil surface adds turbulence near the cells |
| Working volume | Unaffected | Reduced — the coil itself occupies working volume |
| Typical use | Default choice; preferred for shear-sensitive cultures (e.g. animal cells) | Added at large scale when jacket area alone can't remove enough heat, for shear-tolerant microbial cultures |

#### Worked example: evaluating heat generation and removal statements

**Question.** Select the correct statements regarding heat generation and
removal in aerobic bioprocesses: (a) Aerobic microbial growth is
accompanied by heat release proportional to the rate of oxygen consumption.
(b) The heat of fermentation can be estimated using Cooney's correlation:
approximately $460\,\text{kJ}$ per mole of $O_2$ consumed (or
$14\,\text{kJ}$ per g of $O_2$). (c) Scale-up of stirred fermenters creates
heat transfer challenges because reactor volume scales with $D^3$, while
surface area for cooling scales with $D^2$. (d) Internal cooling coils are
preferred over external jackets for highly shear-sensitive animal cell
cultures to minimize volume.

**Solution.**

1. **(a)** Metabolic heat comes from the same respiratory reactions that
   consume $O_2$, so heat release tracks the oxygen uptake rate. Correct.
2. **(b)** This is exactly Cooney's correlation above: $460\,\text{kJ/mol}\
   O_2 \approx 14\,\text{kJ/g}\ O_2$. Correct.
3. **(c)** $V \propto D^3$ and $A \propto D^2$, so specific cooling area
   falls as $1/D$ — the standard scale-up cooling bottleneck. Correct.
4. **(d)** Backwards on both counts: external jackets, not internal coils,
   are preferred for shear-sensitive cultures, precisely because coils add
   internal turbulence/shear and *reduce* usable working volume rather than
   minimizing it. Incorrect.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (c) are correct; (d) is incorrect.

## Key points

- **Conduction** moves heat via vibrational energy or free electrons, with
  no movement of matter; the sign on Fourier's law only tracks direction —
  $\hat{Q}$ itself always comes out positive.
- Heat crossing a jacketed wall moves in three steps: convection (bulk fluid
  to wall), conduction (through the wall), convection (wall to bulk fluid) —
  driven by two separate $\Delta T$s, one per convective step.
- **Nusselt number** ($Nu = hD/k_{fb}$) is the convective-to-conductive heat
  transfer ratio; **Prandtl number** ($Pr = C_p\mu_b/k_{fb}$) is the
  momentum-diffusivity-to-thermal-diffusivity ratio — the calculation chain
  runs $Pr \to Nu \to h \to \hat{Q}$, backwards from what you actually want.
- **Overall heat transfer coefficient** $U$ avoids measuring $\Delta T$ at
  each wall interface: $1/U = 1/h_1 + x/k + 1/h_2$ combines both film
  coefficients with the wall's own conduction resistance.
- $\Delta T_{LMTD} = (\Delta T_2 - \Delta T_1)/\ln(\Delta T_2/\Delta T_1)$.
  For fixed terminal temperatures, $\Delta T_1 + \Delta T_2$ is identical for
  parallel and counter-current flow, but counter-current keeps
  $\Delta T_1$ and $\Delta T_2$ closer together — so its LMTD is always
  $\geq$ parallel flow's, never smaller.
- The two arrangements only tie when one fluid doesn't change temperature at
  all (a condensing vapor or boiling liquid) — otherwise counter-current
  always wins.
- Metabolic heat tracks oxygen consumption — **Cooney's correlation**:
  $\approx 460\,\text{kJ/mol}\ O_2$ ($\approx 14\,\text{kJ/g}\ O_2$).
- Scale-up hurts cooling because $V \propto D^3$ but cooling area $A
  \propto D^2$, so $A/V \propto 1/D$; **external jackets** stay the default
  (and are preferred for shear-sensitive cultures), while **internal
  coils** add area at large scale at the cost of extra shear and working
  volume.
