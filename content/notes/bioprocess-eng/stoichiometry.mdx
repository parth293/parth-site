## General equation of aerobic growth

The **general equation of aerobic growth** balances a carbon source
($C_wH_xO_yN_z$) and a nitrogen source ($H_gO_hN_i$) against oxygen,
forming biomass ($CH_\alpha O_\beta N_\delta$, per mole of carbon — a
"C-mole" basis), carbon dioxide, and water:

$$
C_wH_xO_yN_z + aO_2 + bH_gO_hN_i \rightarrow cCH_\alpha O_\beta N_\delta + dCO_2 + eH_2O
$$

- $w, x, y, z$ — subscripts of the carbon source's molecular formula
- $a$ — stoichiometric coefficient of oxygen (mol)
- $b$ — stoichiometric coefficient of the nitrogen source (mol)
- $g, h, i$ — subscripts of the nitrogen source's molecular formula
- $c$ — stoichiometric coefficient of biomass (mol)
- $\alpha, \beta, \delta$ — subscripts of the biomass formula, on a C-mole
  basis (one carbon atom per formula unit)
- $d$ — stoichiometric coefficient of carbon dioxide (mol)
- $e$ — stoichiometric coefficient of water (mol)

There are five unknown coefficients ($a, b, c, d, e$) but only four
elemental balances (C, H, O, N), so the system is one equation short. That
missing equation has to come from somewhere else — a measured **respiratory
quotient**, a measured biomass yield, or an **available electron balance**
(both covered below).

#### Worked example: aerobic growth on glycine

**Question.** A culture grows aerobically on glycine ($C_2H_5NO_2$) as its
sole carbon and nitrogen source, with ammonia ($NH_3$) as a supplementary
nitrogen source and an assumed cell formula of $CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2}$.
If $1\ \text{mol}$ of biomass forms per mole of glycine consumed, find the
remaining stoichiometric coefficients.

**Solution.**

1. Write the equation with the known subscripts. Glycine gives
   $w=2, x=5, y=2, z=1$; ammonia gives $g=3, h=0, i=1$; biomass is
   $\alpha=1.8, \beta=0.5, \delta=0.2$; and $c = 1$ is given:
   $C_2H_5NO_2 + aO_2 + bNH_3 \rightarrow 1\,CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2} + dCO_2 + eH_2O$.
2. **Carbon balance:** $2 = c + d = 1 + d \Rightarrow d = 1$.
3. **Nitrogen balance:** $1 + b = 0.2c = 0.2(1) = 0.2 \Rightarrow b = -0.8$.
   $b$ comes out negative — glycine alone already supplies more nitrogen
   than $1\ \text{mol}$ of biomass needs, so $0.8\ \text{mol}$ of $NH_3$ is
   actually a **product**, not a reactant.
4. **Hydrogen balance:** $5 + 3b = 1.8c + 2e$. Substituting $b = -0.8$:
   $5 - 2.4 = 1.8 + 2e \Rightarrow 2.6 = 1.8 + 2e \Rightarrow e = 0.4$.
5. **Oxygen balance:** $2 + 2a = 0.5c + 2d + e = 0.5 + 2 + 0.4 = 2.9
   \Rightarrow 2a = 0.9 \Rightarrow a = 0.45$.
6. Check every balance against the final numbers (C: $2 = 1+1$; H:
   $5 = 1.8 + 0.8 + 2.4$; O: $2.9 = 0.5+2+0.4$; N: $1 = 0.2+0.8$) — all hold.

**Answer:**
$C_2H_5NO_2 + 0.45\,O_2 \rightarrow CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2} + CO_2 + 0.4\,H_2O + 0.8\,NH_3$

## Respiratory quotient (RQ)

**Respiratory quotient (RQ)** is the ratio of the rate of carbon dioxide
produced to the rate of oxygen consumed during aerobic metabolism.

$$
RQ = \frac{\text{no. of moles of } CO_2 \text{ produced}}{\text{no. of moles of } O_2 \text{ consumed}}
$$

- $RQ$ — respiratory quotient (ratio of CO$_2$ produced to O$_2$
  consumed — indicates how reduced or oxidized the substrate is: a
  **reduced** substrate is rich in hydrogen relative to oxygen (fats,
  hydrocarbons — still holds a lot of energy/available electrons, so
  needs a lot of $O_2$ to fully combust), while an **oxidized** substrate
  already has more oxygen bonded in and fewer available electrons left to
  give up (organic acids, and $CO_2$ at the fully-oxidized extreme) — see
  degree of reduction, below, for the precise measure of this),
  dimensionless

**Physical significance.** $RQ$ is a direct readout of a culture's
metabolic state, and it's cheap to measure in real time — off-gas analyzers
on a fermenter track $CO_2$ and $O_2$ continuously without needing a liquid
sample. That's why it's used industrially as a live process-monitoring
signal: a shift in $RQ$ can flag a change in metabolic mode (oxidative vs.
fermentative), a substrate switch, or an approaching oxygen limitation,
often before anything else in the run shows it.

| Scenario | Typical $RQ$ | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aerobic growth on a carbohydrate (e.g. glucose → biomass + $CO_2$) | $\approx 1$ | little rearrangement needed between substrate and $CO_2$ |
| Growth on fats or hydrocarbons **as substrate** | $< 1$ | the reduced substrate needs extra $O_2$ per $CO_2$ |
| Growth on organic acids or amino acids as substrate | $> 1$ | an already-oxidized substrate needs less $O_2$ per $CO_2$ |
| **Excretion** of an oxidized organic-acid **product** (citric, oxalic acid) from a carbohydrate | $< 1$ | forming the product itself consumes $O_2$ while releasing little or no $CO_2$ (e.g. $C_6H_{12}O_6 + 1.5O_2 \rightarrow C_6H_8O_7 + 2H_2O$, zero $CO_2$) — this dilutes RQ down, not up |
| **Lipogenesis**: converting carbohydrate **into** neutral lipid (fat synthesis, not fat oxidation) | $> 1$, up to $\approx 1.2\text{–}1.3$ | synthesizing a highly reduced product needs extra NADPH, supplied by decarboxylating pathways (pentose phosphate pathway, malic enzyme) that release $CO_2$ without consuming matching $O_2$ |
| Overflow metabolism on a carbohydrate (byproduct secreted instead of full respiration, e.g. acetate or aerobic ethanol overflow) | slightly $> 1$ | the reduced byproduct branch needs little or no $O_2$ while still releasing $CO_2$ via decarboxylation |
| Pure anaerobic fermentation (e.g. to ethanol) | undefined / $\rightarrow \infty$ | no $O_2$ is consumed at all |

**Don't confuse substrate identity with product identity.** A *reduced
substrate* (fat fed to the cell) needs more $O_2$ per $CO_2$ → $RQ<1$. But
*synthesizing* that same kind of reduced molecule as a product from a
carbohydrate feed has the opposite effect on $RQ$ — it pushes $RQ$ **above**
1, because building reducing power for the product releases $CO_2$ without
matching $O_2$ use. Which one applies depends on whether the reduced
compound is what's being fed in or what's being made.

$RQ$ can also shift **during growth on a fixed substrate**, not just from
substrate choice — if part of the carbon is diverted into a byproduct
("overflow metabolism") instead of going to biomass or full oxidation,
that carbon carries electrons away with it instead of needing $O_2$ to
dispose of them, so $O_2$ consumption drops relative to $CO_2$ evolution
and $RQ$ creeps above $1$ even on a carbohydrate substrate. Two common
real examples:

- **Acetate overflow (*E. coli*)**: at high glycolytic flux, pyruvate is
  produced faster than the TCA cycle/respiratory chain can process it, so
  the excess is excreted as acetate (a mildly oxidized, growth-inhibiting
  byproduct) instead of being fully respired.
- **Crabtree effect (*S. cerevisiae*)**: even with plenty of $O_2$, yeast
  growing fast on excess glucose ferments part of it to ethanol instead of
  fully respiring it ("aerobic fermentation"). Ethanol is a *reduced*
  byproduct, not oxidized, but $RQ$ still rises — diverting carbon away
  from full respiration cuts $O_2$ use more than it cuts $CO_2$ evolution.

The same direction applies to **lipogenesis**: converting carbohydrate
into a highly-reduced storage product like fat pushes $RQ$ **above** $1$
(often measured $1.1$–$1.3$ in indirect calorimetry) — synthesizing a
reduced product needs extra NADPH, generated by decarboxylating pathways
(pentose phosphate pathway) that release $CO_2$ without a matching $O_2$
step, the same mechanism as ethanol overflow above. Excreting an
**oxidized** organic-acid product (citric acid, oxalic acid) instead
consumes $O_2$ to build the extra oxygen into the product while
contributing no matching $CO_2$ (that carbon exits in the acid, not as
$CO_2$) — this pulls $RQ$ **below** $1$, the opposite direction from a
first glance at "oxidized = less $O_2$ needed" might suggest.

| Direction of $\gamma_P$ vs. substrate | Example product | Effect on $RQ$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More reduced ($\gamma_P > \gamma_S$) | ethanol, lipid | pushes $RQ$ **above** 1 |
| More oxidized ($\gamma_P < \gamma_S$) | citric acid, oxalic acid | pushes $RQ$ **below** 1 |

#### Worked example: interpreting an elevated RQ during aerobic growth

**Question.** An aerobic culture converts glucose to biomass and $CO_2$.
The measured $RQ = 1.05$. What does this indicate?

**Solution.**

1. Glucose is a carbohydrate, so the baseline expectation for straightforward
   oxidative growth (biomass + $CO_2$ only) is $RQ \approx 1$.
2. $1.05$ is slightly **above** that baseline, not below — ruling out
   explanations that push $RQ$ **down**: growth on a reduced substrate (fat
   fed in) or excretion of an **oxidized** organic-acid product (citric,
   oxalic acid — consumes $O_2$ to build in oxygen, contributes no matching
   $CO_2$). Also ruling out pure anaerobic fermentation (no $O_2$ term at
   all, so $RQ$ would be undefined/very large, not a small number near 1).
3. A value *above* $1$ on a carbohydrate substrate points to some carbon
   exiting as a **reduced byproduct** (ethanol-type overflow metabolism, or
   net lipogenesis) instead of being fully respired — that pathway releases
   $CO_2$ (via decarboxylation, e.g. pyruvate decarboxylase or the pentose
   phosphate pathway) without a matching $O_2$-consuming step, nudging
   $CO_2$ output up relative to $O_2$ consumed.
4. Nitrogen fixation doesn't touch the carbon/$O_2$ balance at all, so it's
   unrelated to any $RQ$ shift.

**Answer:** Significant overflow metabolism or lipogenesis — carbon
exiting as a *reduced* byproduct, not an oxidized one. (Note: $RQ \approx
1$ is a rounded rule of thumb for carbohydrate substrates, not an exact
value — reading a specific measured $RQ$ this way is a reasonable
exam-level call, but a rigorous diagnosis would need the culture's actual
yield to compute the true expected baseline before judging a deviation.)

## Available electrons

**Available electrons** are the number of electrons a substance can
transfer to oxygen on combustion to $CO_2$, $H_2O$, and $N_2$. Available
electrons can also be considered the valency of each element in the
substance.

| Element | Valency (available e⁻ per atom) |
| --- | --- |
| $C$ | $+4$ |
| $H$ | $+1$ |
| $O$ | $-2$ |
| $P$ | $+5$ |
| $S$ | $+6$ |
| $N$ | variable — depends on the compound |

$N$ has no fixed valency — found the same way as any oxidation state, by
setting the sum of valencies in the compound to zero (or to its charge,
for an ion):

- $-3$ in $NH_3$ or an organic amine
- $0$ in $N_2$
- $+5$ in $NO_3^-$

#### Worked example: available electrons in glycine

**Question.** Find the number of available electrons in glycine
($C_2H_5NO_2$), treating its nitrogen as amino nitrogen (same reference
state as $NH_3$).

**Solution.**

1. Assign valencies: $C = +4$, $H = +1$, $O = -2$, $N = -3$ (amino
   nitrogen).
2. Multiply each valency by the number of atoms of that element:
   $C: 2 \times 4 = 8$; $H: 5 \times 1 = 5$; $O: 2 \times (-2) = -4$;
   $N: 1 \times (-3) = -3$.
3. Sum: $8 + 5 - 4 - 3 = 6$.

**Answer:** Glycine has $6$ available electrons per mole.

## Degree of reduction ($\gamma$)

**Degree of reduction ($\gamma$)** is the number of available electrons in
a substance, normalized per carbon atom.

$$
\gamma = \frac{\text{no. of available electrons}}{\text{no. of carbon atoms}}
$$

- $\gamma$ — degree of reduction (available electrons per carbon atom —
  measures how reduced/energy-rich a substance is; higher $\gamma$ needs
  more $O_2$ to fully oxidize), dimensionless

**Quick reference — valency (available e⁻ per atom):**

| Element / species | Valency |
| --- | --- |
| $C$ | $+4$ |
| $H$ | $+1$ |
| $O$ | $-2$ |
| $P$ | $+5$ |
| $S$ | $+6$ |
| $N$ — amine / $NH_3$ / $NH_2^-$ group | $-3$ |
| $N$ — $N_2$ | $0$ |
| $N$ — $NO_2^-$ (nitrite) | $+3$ |
| $N$ — $NO_3^-$ (nitrate) | $+5$ |

Biomass nitrogen is always at the $-3$ (amine) level regardless of the
nitrogen source fed — see the common confusion note below.

#### Worked example: degree of reduction of glycine

**Question.** Find the degree of reduction of glycine.

**Solution.**

1. Available electrons in glycine $= 6$ (from the worked example above).
2. Glycine has $2$ carbon atoms.
3. $\gamma = 6/2 = 3$.

**Answer:** $\gamma_{\text{glycine}} = 3$.

| Substrate | Formula | $\gamma$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon dioxide | $CO_2$ | $0$ |
| Glycine | $C_2H_5NO_2$ | $3$ |
| Glucose | $C_6H_{12}O_6$ | $4$ |
| Acetic acid | $C_2H_4O_2$ | $4$ |
| Methanol | $CH_4O$ | $6$ |
| Ethanol | $C_2H_6O$ | $6$ |
| Methane | $CH_4$ | $8$ |
| Typical biomass | $CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2}$ | $\approx 4.2$ |

A higher $\gamma$ means the substrate is more reduced (more available
electrons per carbon) — more oxygen is needed to fully oxidize it. $CO_2$
sits at $\gamma = 0$ because it *is* the reference state for carbon — every
other compound's degree of reduction is measured relative to it. $CH_4$, with
no oxygen and the maximum possible hydrogen, is the highest-$\gamma$
single-carbon compound there is.

## Conservation of available electrons

**Available electrons are conserved** in a bioreaction, the same way atoms
are: every electron entering in the carbon source leaves again — in
biomass, in extracellular product, or transferred to $O_2$ as the electron
acceptor. This principle supplies the fifth equation the general growth
equation is missing (see above). Rather than memorizing a symbolic
equation for it, apply the conservation idea directly each time:

> electrons in $=$ electrons in biomass $+$ electrons in product $+$
> electrons to $O_2$ $+$ electrons spent reducing $N$

- Electrons carried by a compound = its degree of reduction $\gamma$
  (electrons per carbon atom) × the moles of carbon it contributes — on a
  C-mole basis, that's just $\gamma \times$ (C-moles present); for a named
  molecule like a product, it's $\gamma \times$ (carbon atoms per molecule)
  × (moles of that molecule).
- **One mole of $O_2$ = $4$ electron equivalents**, from
  $O_2 + 4H^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2H_2O$ — divide the electrons left over
  after biomass and product by $4$ to get moles of $O_2$.
- **Reducing nitrogen costs electrons too**: biomass nitrogen is always
  incorporated at the amine level ($-3$ valency, same as $NH_3$). If the
  feed nitrogen source is more oxidized than that (e.g. $NO_3^-$, $+5$),
  each mole of nitrogen atom reduced down to $-3$ before assimilation costs
  $8$ electrons out of the substrate's budget, before anything reaches
  biomass, product, or $O_2$. This term is $0$ whenever the nitrogen source
  is already at the amine level ($NH_3$ or an amine).

#### Worked example: electron balance with nitrate as the nitrogen source

**Question.** A culture grows aerobically on glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$) using
nitrate ($NO_3^-$) as its sole nitrogen source, forming biomass
$CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2}$. If $0.5\ \text{mol}$ of biomass forms per mole of
glucose consumed, find the moles of $O_2$ consumed per mole of glucose.

**Solution.**

1. Electrons entering with $1\ \text{mol}$ glucose: $\gamma_S \times
   (\text{C atoms}) = 4 \times 6 = 24$ (from the degree-of-reduction table
   above).
2. Degree of reduction of biomass: $\gamma_X = 4(1) + 1.8 - 2(0.5) - 3(0.2)
   = 4.2$ — using amine-nitrogen valency, because that's the oxidation
   state nitrogen sits at once it's inside the cell, regardless of the feed.
3. Electrons landing in biomass: $\gamma_X \times (\text{mol biomass}) =
   4.2 \times 0.5 = 2.1$.
4. Electrons spent reducing nitrogen: the biomass formula has $0.2\ \text{mol
   N}$ per C-mole, and $0.5\ \text{mol}$ biomass forms, so
   $0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1\ \text{mol N}$ reduced, costing
   $8 \times 0.1 = 0.8$ electrons.
5. Electrons left for $O_2$: $24 - 2.1 - 0.8 = 21.1$.
6. Moles of $O_2$: $21.1 / 4 = 5.275$.

**Answer:** $5.275\ \text{mol}\ O_2$ per mole of glucose. Re-running the
same balance with $NH_3$ instead (no nitrogen-reduction cost) gives
$a = (24-2.1)/4 = 5.475\ \text{mol}$ — more $O_2$, not less. $\gamma_X$ was
$4.2$ in both cases; what changed is that nitrate reduction diverted
$0.8$ of the substrate's available electrons away from $O_2$, so less
$O_2$ was needed to balance the rest.

### Working per C-mole of substrate

Cell yield is often reported as $Y_{X/S}$ — **C-mol biomass per C-mol
substrate**, not mole of biomass per mole of substrate. On that basis, one
C-mole of substrate carries exactly $\gamma_S$ electrons (no multiplying by
carbon count needed — that's what "per C-mole" already means), and
$Y_{X/S}$ is directly the moles of biomass to plug into "electrons in
biomass" above.

#### Worked example: oxygen requirement from cell yield and degrees of reduction

**Question.** Yeast grows aerobically on glucose with ammonia as the
nitrogen source. The cell yield $Y_{X/S} = 0.50$ C-mol biomass / C-mol
glucose. The degree of reduction of glucose is $4.00$ and of biomass is
$4.20$. Find the oxygen requirement $a$, in mol $O_2$ per C-mol glucose.

**Solution.**

1. Electrons in, per C-mole of glucose: $\gamma_S = 4.00$.
2. $NH_3$ is the nitrogen source, so nothing is spent reducing nitrogen.
3. Electrons landing in biomass: $\gamma_X \times Y_{X/S} = 4.20 \times
   0.50 = 2.10$.
4. Electrons left for $O_2$: $4.00 - 2.10 = 1.90$.
5. Moles of $O_2$: $1.90 / 4 = 0.475$.

**Answer:** $a = 0.475\ \text{mol}\ O_2$ per C-mol glucose.

### Electron yields ($\eta$)

**Electron yield ($\eta$)** is the fraction of the substrate's available
electrons that end up at a given destination — biomass, $O_2$, or product
— instead of the absolute count used elsewhere in the electron balance.

$$
\eta_X = \frac{\gamma_X c}{\gamma_S w} \qquad\qquad \eta_{O_2} = \frac{4a}{\gamma_S w}
$$

- $\eta_X$ — fraction of substrate electrons transferred to biomass,
  dimensionless
- $\eta_{O_2}$ — fraction of substrate electrons transferred to $O_2$,
  dimensionless
- $\gamma_S w$ — total available electrons entering with the substrate
  (denominator for every $\eta$)

With no product and no nitrogen-reduction cost, dividing the electron
balance $\gamma_S w = \gamma_X c + 4a$ through by $\gamma_S w$ gives
$\eta_X + \eta_{O_2} = 1.0$ — every electron that comes in lands in
biomass or $O_2$, so the two fractions must add to 1 when there's no third
destination.

**A more reduced substrate does not mean a higher $\eta_X$.** Highly
reduced substrates like methane ($\gamma=8$) have a *lower* growth
efficiency per available electron than moderately-reduced substrates like
carbohydrates ($\gamma\approx4$–$4.5$) — methanotrophs waste
proportionally more of the substrate's electrons as heat/to $O_2$. So
$\eta_X$ is lower and $\eta_{O_2}$ is higher for methane, the opposite of
what "more electrons available" might suggest.

#### Worked example: electron yields for growth on glycine

**Question.** Using the glycine worked example above ($\gamma_S=3$,
$\gamma_X=4.2$, $c=1$ mol biomass per mol glycine, $w=2$ carbon atoms),
find $\eta_X$ and $\eta_{O_2}$.

**Solution.**

1. Total substrate electrons: $\gamma_S w = 3 \times 2 = 6$.
2. Electrons to biomass: $\gamma_X c = 4.2 \times 1 = 4.2$, so
   $\eta_X = 4.2/6 = 0.70$.
3. Remaining electrons to $O_2$: $6 - 4.2 = 1.8$, so
   $\eta_{O_2} = 1.8/6 = 0.30$.
4. Check: $\eta_X + \eta_{O_2} = 0.70 + 0.30 = 1.0$ ✓ (no product, and
   glycine's own nitrogen already sits at amine level, so no
   nitrogen-reduction term).

**Answer:** $\eta_X = 0.70$, $\eta_{O_2} = 0.30$ — 70% of glycine's
available electrons are captured in biomass, 30% are spent reducing
$O_2$.

### General equation with product formation

When a cell also excretes an extracellular product, the general growth
equation gains a sixth term:

$$
C_wH_xO_yN_z + aO_2 + bH_gO_hN_i \rightarrow cCH_\alpha O_\beta N_\delta + fC_jH_kO_lN_m + dCO_2 + eH_2O
$$

- $f$ — stoichiometric coefficient of the product (mol)
- $j, k, l, m$ — subscripts of the product's molecular formula — written
  as the real molecule (e.g. $C_2H_6O$ for ethanol), not on a C-mole basis
  the way biomass is

There are now six unknowns ($a, b, c, d, e, f$) but still only four
elemental balances, so two more relations are needed — typically two
measured yields, or one measured yield plus the electron balance below.

### Electron balance with product formation

A product excreted from the cell is a third place electrons can land,
alongside biomass and $O_2$ — its electron content is $\gamma_P \times j$
per mole, where $j$ is carbon atoms per product molecule (needed because,
unlike biomass, a product is written as the real molecule, not on a C-mole
basis).

#### Worked example: fermentation of glucose to ethanol

**Question.** *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* ferments glucose anaerobically to
ethanol and carbon dioxide, with no biomass formed and no oxygen consumed:
$C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow f\,C_2H_5OH + d\,CO_2$. Find $f$ and $d$, and
confirm the result conserves available electrons.

**Solution.**

1. Carbon balance: $6 = 2f + d$.
2. No biomass and no $O_2$ means every electron from the substrate has to
   land in the product instead: electrons in = electrons in product.
3. Electrons in $1\ \text{mol}$ glucose: $\gamma_S \times 6 = 4 \times 6 =
   24$. Electrons per mole of ethanol: $\gamma_P \times j = 6 \times 2 =
   12$ (from the degree-of-reduction table above).
4. $24 = 12f \Rightarrow f = 2$.
5. Substitute into the carbon balance: $6 = 2(2) + d \Rightarrow d = 2$.

**Answer:** $C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2\,C_2H_5OH + 2\,CO_2$ — the classic
fermentation equation. All $24$ of glucose's available electrons land in
ethanol; none go to $CO_2$ ($\gamma = 0$) or $O_2$ (none consumed).

## Yield coefficients

**Yield coefficients** measure how much of the substrate consumed ends up
in biomass or in product, most often reported on a **mass basis** — grams
formed per gram of substrate consumed. This is a different convention from
the C-mole-basis $Y_{X/S}$ used in the electron balance above; both are in
everyday use, so always check which one a number is quoting.

$$
Y_{X/S} = \frac{\Delta X}{\Delta S} \qquad\qquad Y_{P/S} = \frac{\Delta P}{\Delta S}
$$

- $Y_{X/S}$ — biomass yield on substrate (fraction of substrate mass
  converted into biomass), mass basis, in grams of biomass per gram of
  substrate consumed ($\text{g/g}$)
- $Y_{P/S}$ — product yield on substrate (fraction of substrate mass
  converted into product), mass basis, in grams of product per gram of
  substrate consumed ($\text{g/g}$)
- $\Delta X$, $\Delta P$, $\Delta S$ — biomass formed, product formed, and
  substrate consumed, in grams ($\text{g}$)

From a balanced equation, $Y_{X/S} = cMW_X/MW_S$ and $Y_{P/S} = fMW_P/MW_S$,
where $MW_X$, $MW_P$, $MW_S$ are the formula weights of biomass, product,
and substrate.

**Mass yield can exceed $1.0\ \text{g/g}$.** $Y_{P/S}$ only charges
substrate mass in the denominator — it doesn't charge the mass of $O_2$ (or
any other co-reactant) that gets chemically incorporated into the product.
If the product picks up oxygen atoms during synthesis, $MW_P$ can exceed
$MW_S$ per mole converted, pushing $Y_{P/S}$ above $1.0\ \text{g/g}$ without
violating any conservation law — the extra mass came from $O_2$, not $S$.

#### Worked example: mass-basis $Y_{X/S}$ for growth on glycine

**Question.** Using the glycine worked example above ($c = 1$), find the
mass-basis $Y_{X/S}$.

**Solution.**

1. Formula weight of biomass $CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2}$:
   $MW_X = 12.011 + 1.8(1.008) + 0.5(15.999) + 0.2(14.007) =
   24.63\ \text{g/mol}$.
2. Formula weight of glycine $C_2H_5NO_2$:
   $MW_S = 2(12.011) + 5(1.008) + 14.007 + 2(15.999) =
   75.07\ \text{g/mol}$.
3. $Y_{X/S} = cMW_X/MW_S = (1)(24.63)/75.07 = 0.328\ \text{g/g}$.

**Answer:** $Y_{X/S} \approx 0.33\ \text{g biomass/g glycine}$.

## Maximum theoretical yield

**Maximum theoretical yield** is the ceiling on $Y_{X/S}$ or $Y_{P/S}$: the
value reached if every available electron in the substrate landed in
biomass (or in product) with none spent on $O_2$.

### Maximum biomass yield ($Y_{X/S}^{max}$)

If no electrons are spent on $O_2$ or product, all of the substrate's
electrons must land in biomass instead — that's the ceiling, $c_{max}$
(moles of biomass per mole of substrate at the theoretical maximum).

#### Worked example: maximum $Y_{X/S}$ for growth on glycine

**Question.** Find the theoretical maximum biomass yield on glycine, mass
basis.

**Solution.**

1. Electrons in $1\ \text{mol}$ glycine: $\gamma_S \times (\text{C atoms})
   = 3 \times 2 = 6$ (from the degree-of-reduction worked example above).
2. With no $O_2$ and no product, all $6$ electrons must land in biomass:
   $c_{max} = 6/\gamma_X = 6/4.2 = 1.43\ \text{mol biomass/mol glycine}$.
3. $Y_{X/S}^{max} = c_{max}MW_X/MW_S = (1.43)(24.63)/75.07 =
   0.469\ \text{g/g}$.

**Answer:** $Y_{X/S}^{max} \approx 0.47\ \text{g/g}$ — well above the
$0.33\ \text{g/g}$ actually achieved in the worked example above, because
that reaction also spent electrons on $O_2$ ($a = 0.45\ \text{mol}$). This
is a theoretical ceiling, not a target real aerobic growth reaches.

### Maximum product yield ($Y_{P/S}^{max}$)

Same idea, aimed at product instead: if no electrons are spent on $O_2$ or
biomass, all of the substrate's electrons must land in product — the
ceiling, $f_{max}$ (moles of product per mole of substrate at the
theoretical maximum).

#### Worked example: maximum $Y_{P/S}$ for glucose fermented to ethanol

**Question.** Find $Y_{P/S}^{max}$ for ethanol from glucose, and compare it
to the fermentation worked example above.

**Solution.**

1. That fermentation already has $a = 0$ and $c = 0$, so it sits exactly at
   the ceiling: $f_{max} = f = 2$.
2. $MW_P$ (ethanol, $C_2H_6O$) $= 2(12.011) + 6(1.008) + 15.999 =
   46.07\ \text{g/mol}$.
3. $Y_{P/S}^{max} = f_{max}MW_P/MW_S = (2)(46.07)/180.16 =
   0.511\ \text{g/g}$.

**Answer:** $Y_{P/S}^{max} \approx 0.51\ \text{g ethanol/g glucose}$ — the
textbook figure for anaerobic yeast fermentation, and here it's also the
*actual* yield, since the reaction never spends electrons on growth or
$O_2$.

#### Worked example: maximum $Y_{P/S}$ for homolactic fermentation

**Question.** Find $Y_{P/S}^{max}$ (mass basis) for glucose fermented
anaerobically to lactic acid ($C_3H_6O_3$) with no biomass or byproducts.

**Solution.**

1. Balanced equation: $C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2\,C_3H_6O_3$ — glucose and
   $2\times$ lactic acid have the same total atom count, so this is a
   straight isomerization with no $O_2$, $CO_2$, or $H_2O$ involved.
2. Check degree of reduction: glucose $\gamma_S = 4$; lactic acid
   $\gamma_P = [4(3)+6-2(3)]/3 = 12/3 = 4$ — identical, confirming the
   conversion is redox-neutral (no electrons need an $O_2$ or byproduct
   sink).
3. $MW_{glucose} = 180.16\ \text{g/mol}$;
   $2 \times MW_{\text{lactic acid}} = 2(90.08) = 180.16\ \text{g/mol}$ —
   exact match.
4. $Y_{P/S}^{max} = 180.16/180.16 = 1.0\ \text{g/g}$.

**Answer:** $Y_{P/S}^{max} = 1.0\ \text{g lactic acid/g glucose}$ exactly —
when substrate and product share the same degree of reduction, the
conversion is mass-neutral and the theoretical yield is $1.0\ \text{g/g}$
with no computation needed beyond checking $\gamma_S = \gamma_P$.

#### Worked example: maximum molar $Y_{P/S}$ for sucrose to citric acid

**Question.** Find $Y_{P/S}^{max}$ (mol citric acid/mol sucrose) for
sucrose ($C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}$) converted to citric acid ($C_6H_8O_7$),
based strictly on electron conservation.

**Solution.**

1. Available electrons in sucrose: $4(12) + 1(22) - 2(11) = 48$.
2. Available electrons in citric acid: $4(6) + 1(8) - 2(7) = 18$.
3. At the ceiling ($a=0$, $c=0$), all electrons land in product:
   $\gamma_S w = \gamma_P j f \Rightarrow 48 = 18f \Rightarrow f_{max} =
   2.67$.

**Answer:** $Y_{P/S}^{max} = 2.67\ \text{mol citric acid/mol sucrose}$ —
molar yields skip the $MW$ conversion the mass-basis examples above need,
since the electron balance already gives you the mole ratio directly.

## Theoretical oxygen demand (ThOD)

**Theoretical oxygen demand (ThOD)** is the $O_2$ needed to fully oxidize a
substance to $CO_2$, $H_2O$, and $N_2$ — the electron-balance ceiling on
$O_2$ consumption, reached when $c = 0$ and $f = 0$ (no biomass, no
product; every electron goes to $O_2$).

$$
\text{ThOD} = \frac{\text{available electrons per mole}}{4}
$$

- $\text{ThOD}$ — theoretical oxygen demand ($O_2$ needed to fully combust
  the substance — the ceiling on $O_2$ consumption, reached when no
  electrons go to biomass or product), in moles of $O_2$ per mole of
  substance ($\text{mol } O_2/\text{mol}$); multiply by $32\ \text{g/mol}$
  and divide by the molar mass for a mass basis ($\text{g } O_2/\text{g}$)

The converse matters just as much: **any** biomass or extracellular product
formed — even a partially-oxidized one like an organic acid — carries some
of the substrate's electrons away with it, so the actual $O_2$ consumed is
always *less* than ThOD. Complete oxidation to $CO_2$ is the only case that
reaches the ThOD ceiling.

#### Worked example: ThOD of glycine

**Question.** Find the theoretical oxygen demand of glycine, in grams of
$O_2$ per gram of glycine.

**Solution.**

1. Available electrons in glycine $= 6$ (from the worked example above).
2. $\text{ThOD} = 6/4 = 1.5\ \text{mol } O_2/\text{mol glycine}$.
3. Convert to mass: $1.5 \times 32/75.07 = 0.639\ \text{g } O_2/\text{g
   glycine}$.

**Answer:** $\text{ThOD} \approx 0.64\ \text{g } O_2/\text{g glycine}$.

$Y_{X/S}^{max}$, $Y_{P/S}^{max}$, and ThOD are all the same conservation
idea (electrons in = electrons in biomass + electrons in product +
electrons to $O_2$), just with a different pair of destinations zeroed
out — all electrons to biomass, all to product, or all to $O_2$.

## Elemental composition of biomass

A biomass (dry cell) formula written on a C-mole basis —
$CH_\alpha O_\beta N_\delta S_\epsilon P_\zeta$ — gives each element's
**mass contribution** to dry biomass as (subscript × atomic weight),
divided by the total formula weight.

$$
\%\,\text{element}_i = \frac{n_i M_i}{\sum_j n_j M_j} \times 100
$$

- $\%\,\text{element}_i$ — mass contribution of element $i$ to dry biomass
  (what fraction of the cell's dry weight that element accounts for),
  percent ($\%$)
- $n_i$ — subscript (atom count) of element $i$ in the biomass formula,
  dimensionless
- $M_i$ — atomic weight of element $i$, in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$)
- $\sum_j n_j M_j$ — sum of (subscript × atomic weight) over every element
  in the formula, in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$) — the total C-mole
  formula weight

#### Worked example: carbon contribution to yeast dry biomass

**Question.** The elemental composition of the dry biomass of a yeast
species is $CH_{1.6}O_{0.4}N_{0.2}S_{0.0024}P_{0.017}$. Calculate the
contribution of carbon to dry biomass, to two decimal places.

**Solution.**

1. Multiply each subscript by its atomic weight ($C = 12.011$,
   $H = 1.008$, $O = 15.999$, $N = 14.007$, $S = 32.06$, $P = 30.974$):
   - $C: 1 \times 12.011 = 12.011$
   - $H: 1.6 \times 1.008 = 1.613$
   - $O: 0.4 \times 15.999 = 6.400$
   - $N: 0.2 \times 14.007 = 2.801$
   - $S: 0.0024 \times 32.06 = 0.077$
   - $P: 0.017 \times 30.974 = 0.527$
2. Sum all contributions to get the total formula weight:
   $12.011 + 1.613 + 6.400 + 2.801 + 0.077 + 0.527 = 23.43$.
3. Divide carbon's contribution by the total and convert to a percentage:
   $12.011 / 23.43 \times 100 = 51.27\%$.

**Answer:** Carbon contributes $51.27\%$ of dry biomass.

## Key points

- The **general growth equation** has five unknown coefficients but only
  four elemental balances (C, H, O, N) — a fifth relation (measured $RQ$,
  measured yield, or an electron balance) is always needed to solve it.
- If a nitrogen-containing carbon source supplies more nitrogen than the
  biomass formed needs, the nitrogen-source coefficient $b$ comes out
  negative — meaning $NH_3$ is a **product**, not a reactant.
- **Respiratory quotient** $RQ = \dfrac{\text{mol } CO_2}{\text{mol } O_2}$:
  $\approx 1$ for carbohydrates, $< 1$ for **reduced** substrates (fats,
  hydrocarbons), $> 1$ for already-oxidized substrates (organic acids,
  amino acids).
- **Available electrons** = the sum of (valency × atom count) for every
  element in a formula; $C=+4$, $H=+1$, $O=-2$, $P=+5$, $S=+6$ are fixed,
  but $N$'s valency depends on the compound it's found in.
- **Degree of reduction** $\gamma$ = available electrons ÷ number of
  carbon atoms — it measures how reduced a substance is per carbon, and is
  what lets an electron balance replace an elemental balance when solving
  the general growth equation. $CO_2$ is the zero-point ($\gamma=0$); $CH_4$
  is the highest single-carbon value ($\gamma=8$).
- **One mole of $O_2$ = $4$ electron equivalents** — the conversion used to
  turn "electrons left over" into moles of $O_2$ in any electron balance,
  and it follows directly from $O_2 + 4H^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2H_2O$.
- **$\gamma_X$ (biomass) doesn't depend on the nitrogen source** — it's
  fixed by biomass's elemental composition, using amine-nitrogen ($-3$)
  valency, because that's the level nitrogen sits at once it's inside the
  cell either way. Feeding $NO_3^-$ instead of $NH_3$ changes the electron
  balance instead (an extra $8$ electrons per N atom to reduce nitrate down
  to the amine level), which shows up as lower $O_2$ demand, not a lower
  $\gamma_X$.
- A biomass elemental formula is written **per C-mole** (one carbon atom
  per formula unit), so mass-percent composition is just each element's
  (subscript × atomic weight) over the formula's total weight.
- **Product formation adds a sixth term** to the general equation
  ($f\,C_jH_kO_lN_m$) and a matching term to the electron balance
  ($\gamma_P j f$) — the product's electron total needs the $\times j$
  because, unlike biomass, it isn't written on a C-mole basis.
- **Maximum theoretical yield** ($Y_{X/S}^{max}$, $Y_{P/S}^{max}$) is the
  electron balance with $O_2$ zeroed out — all substrate electrons land in
  biomass or product instead. Real aerobic cultures always fall below it,
  since some electrons are always spent on $O_2$.
- **Theoretical oxygen demand (ThOD)** is the same electron balance run the
  other way: $c = f = 0$, every electron goes to $O_2$ — the $O_2$ needed
  to fully combust the substance.

## Common confusion

**Changing the nitrogen source vs. changing $\gamma_X$** — it's tempting to
think that feeding $NO_3^-$ instead of $NH_3$ "uses up" some of nitrogen's
reducing power and lowers biomass's degree of reduction. It doesn't:
$\gamma_X$ is fixed by what's actually in the cell (amine nitrogen, $-3$
valency) and never changes with the feed. The nitrate/ammonia choice only
changes the *electron balance* around growth — nitrate has to be reduced by
$8\ e^-$ per N atom before it can be assimilated, and those electrons come
out of the carbon source's budget instead of going to $O_2$.

**Proof, for $CH_{1.8}O_{0.5}N_{0.2}$ biomass:**
$\gamma_X = 4(1) + 1(1.8) - 2(0.5) - 3(0.2) = 4 + 1.8 - 1 - 0.6 = 4.2$.

Nothing in this line references the feed — the $-3$ is amine-nitrogen
valency, fixed because that's the oxidation state nitrogen has *once it's
in the cell*, not the state it arrived in. So $\gamma_X = 4.2$ whether the
nitrogen source was $NH_3$ or $NO_3^-$.

**C-mole $Y_{X/S}$ vs. mass-basis $Y_{X/S}$** — the same symbol is used for
two different numbers. The C-mole version (C-mol biomass/C-mol substrate,
used in the electron balance) and the mass-basis version (g biomass/g
substrate, used everywhere else) will not match numerically, because they
carry different formula weights in the conversion. For the glycine example
above, $c = 1\ \text{mol biomass/mol glycine}$ is already a molar ratio
(not C-mole, since glycine has $2$ carbons), while $Y_{X/S} = 0.33\
\text{g/g}$ is the same result on a mass basis — always check which basis
a reported $Y_{X/S}$ is on before using it in a calculation.

**"Nitrogen contributes zero available electrons"** — false. Nitrogen's
valency is *variable* (it depends on the compound), which gets mistaken for
*zero*. In biomass specifically it sits at the amine level, $-3$, a real
nonzero (and negative) contribution — it's the $-3\delta$ term in every
$\gamma_X$ calculation above, not an absent one. $N_2$ is the one case
where nitrogen's valency actually is $0$, but that's a special case, not
the general rule.
