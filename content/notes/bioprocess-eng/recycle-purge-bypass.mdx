## Recycle, purge, and bypass streams

**Recycle**, **bypass**, and **purge** are three ways a stream can be routed
around or through a process unit, each solving a different problem: recycle
raises conversion, bypass controls product concentration or temperature, and
purge prevents build-up of unwanted material.

## Recycle

**Recycle** takes part of the product stream and routes it back to combine
with the fresh feed at the process inlet.

![Block diagram: a Feed stream enters a Process box and exits as Product; a Recycling stream splits off from the product stream and feeds back into the process inlet, combining with the fresh feed](/notes/bioprocess-eng/recycle-block-diagram.png)

Reasons to recycle:

- Increase residence time.
- Increase overall conversion.
- But: may reduce productivity beyond a point.

Recycle raises **overall conversion** (of the fresh feed, across the whole
recycle loop) — it does not necessarily raise **single-pass conversion**
(reactor inlet to outlet, one pass). Diluting the reactor's inlet with
partially-converted recycle material typically *lowers* single-pass
conversion; as the recycle ratio grows large, per-pass performance
approaches that of a CSTR (the lowest per-pass conversion for a given
volume), even as overall conversion of fresh feed climbs.

## Cell recycle and washout

**Cell recycle** separates cells out of a bioreactor's outflow (settler,
centrifuge, or membrane) and pumps them back into the tank, so only
cell-free liquid actually leaves — like a sieve that catches marbles while
water drains straight through.

In a normal chemostat, liquid and cells leave at the same rate, so cells
must regrow exactly as fast as they're flushed out. Push the flow rate past
$\mu_{max}$ (the fastest cells can grow) and they're flushed out faster than
they can replace themselves — **washout**.

Cell recycle decouples the two residence times a simple chemostat locks
together:

- **Hydraulic retention time (HRT)** $= V/F = 1/D$ — how long the liquid
  stays in the reactor.
- **Biomass retention time** — how long cells stay, now stretched out
  because the separator returns them instead of letting them leave.

Because cells are held back rather than washed out with the liquid, they no
longer need to grow as fast as the liquid flows through — so the reactor
can run at a dilution rate above $\mu_{max}$ without washing out. The better
the separator concentrates cells back into the recycle stream, the further
above $\mu_{max}$ the flow rate can go before washout.

## Bypass

**Bypass** takes part of the feed stream and routes it around the process
entirely, rejoining the product stream after the process.

![Block diagram: a Feed stream enters a Process box and exits as Product; a Bypass stream splits off from the feed before the process box and rejoins the product stream after the process, without passing through the process](/notes/bioprocess-eng/bypass-block-diagram.png)

Function:

- When the product is too concentrated.
- When temperature control is needed (exothermic process).

## Purge system

**Purge** removes part of the recycle stream from the system entirely,
rather than returning all of it to the feed.

![Block diagram: a Feed stream enters a Process box and exits as Product; a Recycle stream splits off from the product stream, and before rejoining the feed a portion of it is diverted as a Purge stream leaving the system](/notes/bioprocess-eng/purge-system-block-diagram.png)

Function:

- If an inert or impurity enters with the feed or forms inside the reactor,
  recycling it can cause its build-up — a portion of the recycle must be
  purged to prevent this.

At steady state, whatever inert or impurity enters with the fresh feed has
nowhere to go but out with the purge — the recycle stream itself is
internal to the loop and doesn't add or remove any of it. That balance is
what sets how large the purge needs to be for a given feed impurity level
and a given allowed build-up in the loop.

## Recycle vs. bypass vs. purge

| | Splits from | Rejoins | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recycle | Product stream | Feed, before the process | Increase residence time and overall conversion |
| Bypass | Feed stream | Product, after the process | Control product concentration; temperature control for exothermic processes |
| Purge | Recycle stream | Leaves the system (does not rejoin) | Prevent build-up of inerts or impurities |

## Key points

- **Recycle** returns part of the product stream to the feed to increase
  residence time and overall conversion — but pushed too far, it can reduce
  productivity.
- Recycle raises **overall** conversion of fresh feed, not necessarily
  **single-pass** conversion — per-pass performance typically drops toward
  CSTR-like behavior as the recycle ratio grows.
- **Cell recycle** decouples hydraulic retention time ($1/D$) from biomass
  retention time — returning cells that would otherwise wash out lets a
  bioreactor run at a dilution rate above $\mu_{max}$ without washing out.
- **Bypass** diverts part of the feed around the process and rejoins it with
  the product, used when the product is too concentrated or when
  temperature control is needed for an exothermic process.
- **Purge** bleeds off part of the recycle stream so inerts or impurities
  entering with the feed, or forming inside the reactor, don't build up.

## Common confusion

Recycle, bypass, and purge all add a second stream around the process box,
but they connect at different points: recycle splits from the **product**
and rejoins the **feed**; bypass splits from the **feed** and rejoins the
**product**, skipping the process; purge splits from the **recycle** stream
and leaves the system rather than rejoining anything.

**Overall conversion vs. single-pass conversion** — "recycle increases
conversion" is true for **overall** conversion (fresh feed in, unreacted
material eventually recovered and reacted), but recycle typically *lowers*
**single-pass** conversion (just reactor inlet to outlet), because the
reactor inlet is diluted with partially-converted recycle material instead
of pure fresh feed. These are not the same number, and a claim that recycle
"always increases conversion across the reactor vessel" is conflating them.
