## Energy and work

**Thermodynamics** studies energy transformations in the universe and tells
us whether a given physical process is possible.

### Energy

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. One newton-metre is one joule
(1 N·m = 1 J).

A **calorie** (cal) is the heat energy needed to raise the temperature of
1 gram of water by 1 °C; 1 cal = 4.184 J. A **kilocalorie** (kcal) is 1000
calories: 1 kcal = 1000 cal = 4184 J.

### Work

**Work** can be defined two ways.

#### Force–displacement form

$$
W = Fd
$$

- $W$ — work, joule (J)
- $F$ — force, newton (N)
- $d$ — displacement, metre (m)

#### Pressure–volume form

$$
W = P\Delta V
$$

- $W$ — work, joule (J)
- $P$ — pressure, pascal (Pa)
- $\Delta V$ — change in volume, cubic metre (m³)

## System, surroundings, and universe

**System** is the matter within a specific region of space under
consideration. **Surroundings** is the matter in the rest of the universe.
System plus surroundings equals the universe.

### Types of system

| System type | Mass transfer | Energy transfer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | Yes | Yes |
| Closed | No | Yes |
| Isolated | No | No |

## State functions

**State functions** are properties that depend only on the initial and
final state of the system, not on the path the system follows between
them.

**Pressure**, **volume**, **internal energy**, **enthalpy**, **entropy**,
and **free energy** are all state functions. Because a state function's
value depends only on the state, its change is written with $\Delta$ — for
example $\Delta U$ for the change in internal energy.

**Work** and **heat** depend on the path the system follows, not just its
initial and final state, so they are **path functions** — non-state
functions.

| | State function | Path function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depends on | Initial and final state only | Path taken |
| Examples | Pressure, volume, internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, free energy | Work, heat |
| Change written as | $\Delta X$ | Not written with $\Delta$ |

## Zeroth law of thermodynamics

The **zeroth law of thermodynamics** states that if system A is in
thermal equilibrium with system B, and system B is in thermal equilibrium
with system C, then system A is also in thermal equilibrium with system C.

Two systems are in **thermal equilibrium** when no net heat flows between
them — they are at the same temperature. The zeroth law is what makes
temperature a well-defined, measurable property: a thermometer (system B)
can be brought into equilibrium with A, then with C, and if both read the
same temperature, A and C are in equilibrium with each other without ever
being placed in contact.

## First law of thermodynamics

The **first law of thermodynamics** states that the total energy of a
system and its surroundings is constant.

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed — it can only be transformed
from one form to another. This is the **law of conservation of energy**.

## Internal energy

**Internal energy** is the total energy contained within a system. It is
a state function, and it can change only if energy flows into or out of
the system as heat or work.

$$
\Delta U = U_2 - U_1
$$

- $\Delta U$ — change in internal energy, joule (J)
- $U_2$ — final internal energy of the system, joule (J)
- $U_1$ — initial internal energy of the system, joule (J)

Internal energy also changes according to how heat and work cross the
system boundary:

$$
\Delta U = \pm q \pm w
$$

- $q$ — heat transferred across the system boundary, joule (J)
- $w$ — work transferred across the system boundary, joule (J)

![Box labeled system with heat q and work w arrows shown entering and leaving, next to the equation delta U equals plus-minus q plus-minus w and the sign convention list](/notes/bioprocess-eng/first-law-sign-convention-diagram.png)

- $+q$ — heat gained by the system
- $-q$ — heat lost by the system
- $+w$ — work done on the system
- $-w$ — work done by the system

At constant volume, $\Delta V = 0$, so no pressure–volume work is done and
the first law reduces to $\Delta U = q_v$ — the change in internal energy
equals the heat exchanged at constant volume.

## Enthalpy

In biological systems, volume is not constant — pressure is constant
instead. This is why **enthalpy** is introduced: it is the heat exchanged
at constant pressure.

When volume changes at constant pressure, part of the energy released
goes into pushing back the surroundings — the $P\Delta V$ term — energy
spent, but not felt as heat. Enthalpy is internal energy plus that
unavoidable boundary-pushing work, so $\Delta H$ is the part left over as
heat a thermometer or calorimeter actually detects. This is why a vented
bioreactor, whose pressure stays roughly constant while its volume does
not, is tracked by enthalpy rather than internal energy.

$$
\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V
$$

- $\Delta H$ — change in enthalpy, joule (J)
- $\Delta U$ — change in internal energy, joule (J)
- $P$ — pressure, pascal (Pa)
- $\Delta V$ — change in volume, cubic metre (m³)

At constant pressure, $\Delta H = q_p$ — the change in enthalpy equals the
heat exchanged at constant pressure. A positive $\Delta H$ is
**endothermic** (the system absorbs heat); a negative $\Delta H$ is
**exothermic** (the system releases heat).

If both pressure and volume are constant, $\Delta V = 0$, so
$P\Delta V = 0$ and $\Delta H = \Delta U$.

Enthalpy is itself a state function, because it is built from a
combination of other state functions — internal energy, pressure, and
volume.

#### Worked example: enthalpy vs. internal energy in a vented bioreactor

**Question.** An aerobic fermentation runs in a bioreactor vented to hold
a constant headspace pressure of 101.3 kPa (1 atm). Over one hour, the
culture's respiration is exothermic enough that the cooling jacket has to
remove 850 kJ of heat to hold temperature — so $q_p = -850$ kJ. Off-gas
analysis shows CO$_2$ production exceeds O$_2$ consumption enough that the
gas phase expands by 50 L ($5.0 \times 10^{-2}$ m³) over that hour. Find
$\Delta U$ for the hour, and compare it with $\Delta H$.

**Solution.**
1. At constant pressure, $\Delta H = q_p = -850$ kJ.
2. Compute the pressure–volume work term:
   $P\Delta V = 101{,}300\ \text{Pa} \times 0.050\ \text{m}^3 = 5{,}065\ \text{J} \approx 5.1$ kJ.
3. Rearrange $\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V$ to solve for $\Delta U$:
   $\Delta U = \Delta H - P\Delta V = -850 - 5.1 = -855.1$ kJ.

**Answer:** $\Delta U \approx -855.1$ kJ, compared with $\Delta H = -850$
kJ — a difference of about 5 kJ, or roughly 0.6% of the total.

That 5 kJ is real energy: it is what left the system as gas-phase
expansion work against the vent, not as heat the jacket could remove.
It is small here because the gas volume change is small relative to the
heat released, but it is exactly the term you would need to close an
energy balance around the headspace or size an off-gas system — and in a
sealed, gas-generating process where $\Delta V$ is larger, it stops being
a rounding error.

### Bond energy

**Bond energy** (or bond enthalpy) is the energy required to break a
bond — breaking a bond is endothermic. Forming a bond releases energy —
bond formation is exothermic. Bond energy is itself an enthalpy term.

## Entropy

**Entropy** is a measure of randomness, disorder, or chaos in a system.
Systems with low entropy are structured and ordered; systems with high
entropy are less structured.

Examples of increasing entropy:

- Ice → water — a fixed, ordered crystal lattice becomes a disordered
  liquid; entropy increases.
- Starch → glucose — one large ordered polymer breaks into many small,
  freely moving molecules; entropy increases.
- Glucose → carbon dioxide and water — one molecule becomes several
  simpler, more freely moving molecules; entropy increases.
- Folded protein → unfolded protein — a defined three-dimensional
  structure unravels into a disordered chain; entropy increases.

Ecological succession also illustrates changing structure over time:
**pioneer** (or founder) **community** → **seral community** →
**climax community** — a sparse, simple community develops into a
complex, highly structured ecosystem, so entropy decreases (order
increases) as succession proceeds.

### Entropy in biochemical systems

**Bond order.** Going from single bonds to double bonds to triple bonds,
entropy decreases — a single bond allows free rotation around it, while
double and triple bonds are progressively more rigid and restrict
molecular motion.

**Monomers versus polymers.** A polymer is more ordered than its free
monomers, so it has lower entropy — glycogen (a polymer) has lower
entropy than free glucose (its monomer).

## Second law of thermodynamics

The **second law of thermodynamics** states that the total entropy of a
system and its surroundings always increases for an irreversible
process.

Naturally occurring processes proceed toward **equilibrium** — a state of
minimum potential energy — and systems tend to flow from ordered states
toward disordered states.

$$
\Delta S = S_2 - S_1
$$

- $\Delta S$ — change in entropy, joule per kelvin (J/K)
- $S_2$ — final entropy of the system, joule per kelvin (J/K)
- $S_1$ — initial entropy of the system, joule per kelvin (J/K)

For a spontaneous (irreversible) process, $\Delta S > 0$.

### Biological systems and the second law

Biological systems are not exceptions to the second law. When part of a
system appears to become more ordered — a growing cell assembling
disordered nutrients into ordered biomass, for instance — the entropy of
the surroundings increases by more than enough to compensate, so the
total entropy of system plus surroundings still increases.

#### Worked example: does a growing cell violate the second law?

**Question.** Over one hour, a growing microbial culture assembles
disordered nutrients (glucose, amino acids) into ordered biomass,
decreasing the system's entropy by $\Delta S_{sys} = -2.5$ kJ/K. In that
same hour, the culture's respiration releases 850 kJ of heat to the
surroundings (the reactor's cooling water), held at a constant
$T = 310$ K (37 °C). Find $\Delta S_{surr}$ and $\Delta S_{universe}$, and
check whether the second law holds.

**Solution.**
1. For a large reservoir at constant temperature, heat released by the
   system raises the surroundings' entropy by $\Delta S_{surr} = q / T$.
2. $\Delta S_{surr} = \dfrac{850{,}000\ \text{J}}{310\ \text{K}} \approx 2{,}742\ \text{J/K} \approx 2.74$ kJ/K.
3. $\Delta S_{universe} = \Delta S_{sys} + \Delta S_{surr} = -2.5 + 2.74 = +0.24$ kJ/K.

**Answer:** $\Delta S_{universe} \approx +0.24$ kJ/K — positive, so the
second law holds. The cell's own entropy fell, but the heat it released
into the surroundings raised their entropy by more, so the total for
system plus surroundings still increased.
