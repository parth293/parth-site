## Mass transfer by diffusion

Mass transfer inside a bioreactor happens through diffusion.

### Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the thermal motion of fluid particles at any temperature
above absolute zero — 0 K, or −273 °C.

### Fick's law of diffusion

**Fick's law of diffusion** states that molecules move from areas of higher
concentration to areas of lower concentration.

![Dense cluster of component A particles at higher concentration on the left, with arrows passing through a circular cross-sectional area toward a sparser cluster of the same particles at lower concentration on the right](/notes/bioprocess-eng/fick-law-diffusion-flux-diagram.png)

Mass transfer of component A across a cross-sectional area perpendicular to
the direction of diffusion can be explained by this law.

$$
J_A = \frac{N_A}{a} = -D_{AB}\frac{dC_A}{dy}
$$

- $J_A$ — mass flux of component A, in kilograms per square metre-second
  ($\text{kg/(m}^2 \cdot \text{s)}$)
- $N_A$ — rate of mass transfer of component A, in kilograms per second
  ($\text{kg/s}$)
- $a$ — cross-sectional area, perpendicular to the direction of diffusion, in
  square metres ($\text{m}^2$) — not to be confused with the subscript $A$,
  which denotes the diffusing component
- $D_{AB}$ — diffusion coefficient of A through B, describing the ease of
  diffusion, in square metres per second ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
- $C_A$ — mass concentration of component A, in kilograms per cubic metre
  ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
- $y$ — distance in the direction of diffusion, in metres ($\text{m}$)

**The negative sign in the equation is because diffusion occurs from higher
concentration to lower concentration.**

## Film theory

**Film theory** states that at the interface between two phases, there
exists a thin, stagnant film or layer of fluid. Within this film, mass
transfer occurs only via molecular diffusion.

For example, when a bubble enters a bioreactor, a thin film of the same
bulk fluid surrounds the bubble. This film makes it difficult for gas
inside the bubble to diffuse out of the bubble and into the fluid.

### Concentration profile across the film

![Concentration profile of solute A across two phases in contact — organic phase bulk fluid on the left, aqueous phase bulk fluid on the right, separated by a phase boundary flanked by an organic film and an aqueous film; a curve shows concentration falling from CA1 in the aqueous bulk fluid, through CA1i and CA2i at the phase boundary, down to CA2 in the organic bulk fluid](/notes/bioprocess-eng/film-theory-concentration-profile-interface.png)

A solute moves from the bulk fluid on one side, across the phase boundary,
into the bulk fluid on the other side:

1. In the bulk fluid of the first phase (e.g. the aqueous phase), the
   solute is at concentration $C_{A1}$.
2. Moving through that phase's film toward the phase boundary, the
   concentration drops to $C_{A1i}$ (the interface concentration on this
   side) — the film poses resistance to transfer, since diffusion is the
   only way for the solute to move through it.
3. At the phase boundary itself, there is no transfer resistance —
   instantaneous thermodynamic equilibrium is established. The
   concentration can rise or drop here, from $C_{A1i}$ to $C_{A2i}$,
   because the solubility of the solute differs between the two phases —
   the partition coefficient decides the concentration on each side.
4. Moving across the second phase's film (e.g. the organic phase), the
   concentration continues to drop, from $C_{A2i}$ down to $C_{A2}$ in
   that phase's bulk fluid.

Overall: $C_{A1} > C_{A1i} > C_{A2i} > C_{A2}$.

Within each film, the concentration profile is **linear**. Film theory
assumes steady-state molecular diffusion through the film with no
accumulation, and integrating Fick's law under that assumption gives a
straight-line concentration gradient — the same reasoning that gives a
linear temperature profile across a wall in steady-state heat conduction.

### Rate of mass transfer (film theory)

Based on film theory, the mass transfer rate is:

$$
N_A = k_L\, a\, (C^* - C)
$$

- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of mass transfer, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $k_L$ — (liquid) film mass-transfer coefficient, in metres per second
  ($\text{m/s}$)
- $a$ — interfacial area per unit volume, in square metres per cubic
  metre ($\text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$)
- $C^*$ — saturation concentration: the maximum concentration of the
  solute that can dissolve in the liquid, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C$ — bulk concentration: the current concentration of the solute in the
  liquid, in gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

$k_L$ is how fast the molecule crosses the film: the thinner the film, the
higher $k_L$, and the faster the transfer.

$C^* - C$ is the driving force for mass transfer: the bigger the
difference between the saturation concentration and the bulk
concentration, the more strongly the system pulls the solute into the
liquid.

### Interfacial area per unit volume ($a$)

**Interfacial area per unit volume** ($a$) is the total interfacial area
between the two phases, divided by the total volume of the liquid (the
bulk liquid) in the vessel — not the total vessel volume including the gas
phase. In practice, the gas phase (bubbles) occupies only a small fraction
of the vessel, so the liquid volume is used as the "total volume" in this
ratio.

For a sphere, $a = S/V = 6/d$ — so smaller bubbles mean higher $a$. Splitting
a fixed total gas volume into $n$ equal bubbles multiplies $a$ (and the
total interfacial area) by $n^{1/3}$. This is why sparging aims to break gas
into fine bubbles rather than large ones.

#### Worked example: interfacial area from bubble size

**Question.** A sparger releases a fixed volume of gas as a single bubble of
diameter $10\ \text{mm}$. What is the interfacial area per unit volume ($a$)
for (a) that single bubble, and (b) the same total gas volume split instead
into 100 equal smaller bubbles?

**Solution.**

1. For a sphere, $a = 6/d$.
2. Single bubble: $d = 10\ \text{mm} = 0.01\ \text{m}$, so
   $a = 6/0.01 = 600\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$.
3. Splitting a fixed total gas volume into $n$ equal bubbles scales $a$ by
   $n^{1/3}$ (smaller diameter, more total surface for the same volume).
4. For $n = 100$: $a = 600 \times 100^{1/3} \approx 600 \times 4.64 \approx
   2790\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$.

**Answer:** $a \approx 600\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$ for the single bubble,
rising to $\approx 2790\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$ once split into 100 smaller
bubbles of the same total volume.

### Gas hold-up ($\varepsilon_g$)

**Gas hold-up** ($\varepsilon_g$) is the fraction of the gas-liquid
dispersion's volume occupied by gas bubbles at any instant — not the
sparging flow rate, but how much gas is actually held up inside the vessel
while it rises through the liquid.

$$
\varepsilon_g = \frac{V_g}{V_g + V_L}
$$

- $\varepsilon_g$ — gas hold-up, dimensionless (fraction, $0$–$1$)
- $V_g$ — volume of gas (bubbles) in the dispersion, in cubic metres
  ($\text{m}^3$)
- $V_L$ — volume of liquid in the dispersion, in cubic metres ($\text{m}^3$)

$\varepsilon_g$ is what turns single-bubble geometry ($6/d$ for one sphere)
into a vessel-level interfacial area. The general form folds it in
directly:

$$
a = \frac{6\varepsilon_g}{d_b}
$$

- $a$ — interfacial area per unit volume, in square metres per cubic metre
  ($\text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$)
- $\varepsilon_g$ — gas hold-up, dimensionless
- $d_b$ — Sauter mean bubble diameter, in metres ($\text{m}$)

The single-bubble worked example above used $a = 6/d$ as shorthand for a
*fixed* total gas volume — that special case works because $\varepsilon_g$
is the same in both scenarios being compared, so it cancels out. For any
absolute calculation, use the full $a = 6\varepsilon_g/d_b$.

**What raises $\varepsilon_g$ (and generally $a$):**
- **Higher superficial gas (sparging) velocity** — more gas fed per unit
  time and cross-section means more gas volume held up in the liquid at
  any instant.
- **Smaller bubbles** — they rise more slowly, so they spend longer in the
  liquid before escaping, raising hold-up for the same gas flow rate.

**What lowers it:**
- **Coalescence** — bubbles merging into larger ones increases $d_b$
  directly and makes bubbles rise faster (less residence time), lowering
  both $\varepsilon_g$ and $a$. Coalescence always works against
  interfacial area, never for it.
- **High viscosity** — thick, non-Newtonian broths often promote
  coalescence and slow bubble breakup, generally reducing hold-up and $a$.

**Electrolytes — two separate effects, easy to conflate.** Dissolved
salts (e.g. NaCl, Na$_2$SO$_4$) do two unrelated things in a sparged
system:

- **They inhibit bubble coalescence.** Ions in solution stabilize the
  thin liquid film between two approaching bubbles, preventing them from
  merging. This keeps mean bubble diameter smaller and $a$ (and $k_La$)
  higher than in pure water, at the same gas flow rate.
- **They lower O$_2$ solubility ($C^*$) via salting-out** (see "Oxygen
  solubility" below) — a completely separate mechanism (ionic strength
  suppressing gas solubility, not bubble size).

So electrolytes raise $a$/$k_La$ (via smaller, more stable bubbles) while
simultaneously *lowering* $C^*$ (via salting-out) — two different
variables, two different mechanisms, pulling in directions that don't
cancel out predictably. There is no "salting-in" effect that raises
O$_2$ solubility here — that term describes a different phenomenon
(certain organic solutes becoming *more* soluble with certain salts), not
this case.

#### Worked example: electrolyte effects in a sparged bioreactor

**Question.** Which of the following effects occur when pure
electrolytes (salts such as NaCl or Na$_2$SO$_4$) are added to pure water
in a sparged bioreactor? (a) Electrolytes inhibit bubble coalescence.
(b) Mean bubble diameter decreases at high salt concentrations.
(c) Interfacial area $a$ increases. (d) Oxygen solubility $C^*$ increases
due to the salting-in effect.

**Solution.**

1. Ions stabilize the thin film between approaching bubbles, preventing
   them from merging — (a) is correct.
2. Coalescence inhibition means bubbles stay smaller instead of merging
   into larger ones as salt concentration rises — (b) is correct.
3. Smaller $d_b$ at roughly the same hold-up means $a = 6\varepsilon_g/d_b$
   rises — (c) is correct.
4. Electrolytes lower O$_2$ solubility via **salting-out**, not raise it
   via "salting-in" — (d) gets both the direction and the name of the
   effect wrong.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (c) are correct; (d) is false.

#### Worked example: which statements about interfacial area are correct

**Question.** Select the CORRECT statements regarding the gas-liquid
interfacial area per unit volume $a$: (a) $a$ is related to gas hold-up
($\varepsilon_g$) and bubble diameter ($d_b$) by $a = 6\varepsilon_g/d_b$.
(b) High shear from Rushton turbines reduces mean bubble diameter, thereby
increasing $a$. (c) Increasing gas sparging velocity increases gas
hold-up, generally increasing $a$. (d) Coalescence of air bubbles
increases $a$.

**Solution.**

1. (a) is exactly the general interfacial-area equation above — correct.
2. Rushton turbines are high-shear radial impellers; that shear breaks
   bubbles into smaller ones, and since $a \propto 1/d_b$, a smaller $d_b$
   means a higher $a$ — (b) is correct.
3. Higher sparging (superficial gas) velocity increases $\varepsilon_g$
   (more gas held up in the dispersion at any instant); with $d_b$ roughly
   unchanged, $a = 6\varepsilon_g/d_b$ rises too — (c) is correct.
4. Coalescence merges small bubbles into larger ones, increasing $d_b$.
   Since $a \propto 1/d_b$, this **decreases** $a$ — (d) is false; it
   directly contradicts (b) and (c), which both work by keeping bubbles
   small.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (c) are correct; (d) is false.

### Volumetric mass transfer coefficient ($k_L a$)

$k_L$ and $a$ are hard to measure separately, so they are lumped into a
single **volumetric mass transfer coefficient**:

$$
N_A = k_L a\, (C^* - C)
$$

- $k_L a$ — volumetric mass transfer coefficient, in reciprocal seconds
  ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of mass transfer, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $C^*$ — saturation concentration, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C$ — bulk concentration, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

$k_L$ and $a$ change together (e.g. faster impeller speed raises both),
so they're rarely separated in practice. In aerobic fermentation, $A$
is oxygen, so $k_L a$ represents the bioreactor's **oxygen transfer
capacity** — the standard parameter for bioreactor design and scale-up.

## Oxygen uptake in a bioreactor

### Oxygen solubility ($C^*$) and Henry's law

**Oxygen is a sparingly soluble gas** in aqueous media — at
$25\,^\circ\text{C}$, water in equilibrium with ambient air holds only
about $7$–$9\ \text{mg/L}$ dissolved O$_2$ ($\approx 0.2$–
$0.3\ \text{mmol/L}$). This small number is exactly why $k_L a$ has to be
so large in aerobic fermentation: the absolute driving force
$(C^*_{AL} - C_{AL})$ available at any moment is tiny.

Henry's law sets $C^*$ from the partial pressure of oxygen in the gas
phase:

$$
C^*_{AL} = \frac{p_{O_2}}{H}
$$

- $C^*_{AL}$ — saturation concentration of oxygen in the liquid, in
  gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $p_{O_2}$ — partial pressure of oxygen in the gas phase, in pascals
  ($\text{Pa}$)
- $H$ — Henry's law constant for oxygen in the broth, in
  pascal-cubic-metres per gram-mole ($\text{Pa}\cdot\text{m}^3/\text{gmol}$)

**What raises $C^*$:**
- **Higher headspace/total pressure** — for a fixed O$_2$ mole fraction in
  the gas, raising total pressure raises $p_{O_2}$ proportionally, so
  $C^*$ rises. This is why some high-cell-density fermentations run
  pressurized: it raises the achievable driving force without needing
  more $k_L a$.

**What lowers $C^*$:**
- **Higher temperature** — gas dissolution is exothermic, so $C^*$ falls
  as temperature rises (see Temperature under "Factors affecting $k_L a$"
  below) — pulling in the opposite direction from higher headspace
  pressure.
- **Salting-out effect** — high concentrations of dissolved electrolytes
  and sugars in the broth raise its ionic strength, which lowers gas
  solubility. Concentrated fermentation media can measurably suppress
  $C^*$ below the value for pure water.

#### Worked example: oxygen solubility claims

**Question.** Select the CORRECT statements regarding oxygen solubility
in aqueous fermentation broth: (a) O$_2$ is a sparingly soluble gas, with
$C^*$ typically around $7$–$9\ \text{mg/L}$ at $25\,^\circ\text{C}$ under
ambient air. (b) Increasing bioreactor temperature increases $C^*$.
(c) Increasing vessel headspace pressure increases $C^*$ according to
Henry's law. (d) High concentrations of dissolved electrolytes and sugars
decrease $C^*$ via the salting-out effect.

**Solution.**

1. (a) is the standard textbook solubility value for O$_2$ in water at
   room temperature under air — correct.
2. Gas dissolution is exothermic, so solubility falls as temperature
   rises, not rises — (b) is false.
3. Henry's law, $C^*_{AL} = p_{O_2}/H$, makes $C^*$ directly proportional
   to the partial pressure of O$_2$; raising headspace pressure raises
   $p_{O_2}$ and therefore $C^*$ — (c) is correct.
4. Dissolved electrolytes and sugars raise the broth's ionic strength,
   which suppresses gas solubility (salting-out) — (d) is correct.

**Answer:** (a), (c), and (d) are correct; (b) is false.

### Rate of oxygen transfer

$$
N_A = k_L a\, (C^*_{AL} - C_{AL})
$$

- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of oxygen transfer (OTR), in gram-mole per
  cubic metre per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $k_L a$ — volumetric mass transfer coefficient, in reciprocal seconds
  ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $C^*_{AL}$ — saturation concentration of oxygen in the liquid (its
  maximum solubility), in gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C_{AL}$ — instantaneous concentration of oxygen in the liquid, in
  gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

![Path of an oxygen molecule from a gas bubble to a cell's reaction site, numbered in eight steps: (1) interior of the bubble to the gas-liquid interface, (2) across the gas-liquid interface, (3) through the liquid film around the bubble, (4) across the bulk liquid, (5) through the liquid film around the cell, (6) across the liquid-cell interface, (7) to an individual cell within a clump, (8) through the cytoplasm to the reaction site — with k_L a labeling the overall path](/notes/bioprocess-eng/oxygen-transfer-resistance-steps.png)

Oxygen travels from a gas bubble to a cell's reaction site through eight
resistances in series:

1. Transfer from the interior of the bubble to the gas-liquid interface
2. Transfer across the gas-liquid interface
3. Diffusion through the liquid film around the bubble
4. Transport through the bulk liquid
5. Diffusion through the liquid film around the cell
6. Movement across the liquid-cell interface
7. For cells in a clump, diffusion to the individual cell
8. Transport through the cytoplasm to the reaction site

**Step 3 — diffusion through the liquid film around the bubble — is the
largest resistance**, which is why $k_L a$ (built around that film) is
the parameter that governs overall oxygen transfer.

This holds for **free unicellular cells in well-mixed suspension**, and
rests on two other facts about the eight steps:

- **Step 2 (gas-film resistance) is negligible.** O$_2$ diffuses roughly
  four orders of magnitude faster in the gas phase than in the liquid
  phase, so for a sparingly-soluble gas like O$_2$ almost all the
  resistance sits on the liquid side of the interface — this is why only
  the liquid-film coefficient ($k_L$) appears in $k_L a$, with no matching
  gas-film term.
- **Step 5 (liquid film around the cell) is negligible for small, free
  cells.** A single bacterium or yeast cell is small enough, and its own
  mass-transfer coefficient high enough, that this film contributes
  almost nothing next to step 3 — don't confuse it with the bubble's film.

**Exception: mycelial pellets.** For filamentous fungi growing as pellets
or clumps (step 7 in the list), O$_2$ must additionally diffuse through
the biomass matrix to reach cells at the core. Past a critical pellet
radius, this **intra-pellet diffusion resistance** dominates instead —
large enough pellets can starve their core of oxygen entirely (an anoxic
core), even when $k_L a$ at the bubble is perfectly adequate. So "the
liquid film around the bubble is always the dominant resistance" is only
true for free-cell suspensions, not pelleted or flocculated growth.

#### Worked example: which resistance dominates, and when

**Question.** Which of the following statements about O$_2$ transfer
resistances from a gas bubble to a microbial cell in a stirred bioreactor
are correct? (a) For unicellular bacteria in well-mixed suspension, the
primary resistance is the liquid film around the gas bubble. (b) Gas-film
resistance is practically negligible. (c) For fungal mycelial pellets,
intra-pellet O$_2$ diffusion is a significant resistance. (d) The liquid
film around an individual bacterial cell is the dominant resistance for
all aerobic bioprocesses.

**Solution.**

1. For free unicellular cells, step 3 (liquid film around the bubble) is
   the largest of the eight resistances — (a) is correct.
2. O$_2$'s gas-phase diffusivity is far higher than its liquid-phase
   diffusivity, so the gas film (step 2) contributes negligible resistance
   — (b) is correct.
3. Mycelial pellets add an extra resistance step not present for free
   cells: diffusion through the pellet's own biomass to reach interior
   cells, which becomes dominant past a critical pellet size — (c) is
   correct.
4. (d) conflates two different films: the film around the *cell* (step 5,
   negligible for small free cells) with the film around the *bubble*
   (step 3, the actual dominant resistance) — and ignores that pellets
   shift the dominant resistance elsewhere entirely. "For all aerobic
   bioprocesses" is false.

**Answer: (a), (b), and (c) are correct; (d) is false.**

### Factors affecting cellular oxygen demand

1. Species of bacteria consuming the oxygen
2. Growth phase the cells are in
3. Nature of the carbon source fed to the cells

### Rate of oxygen consumption per cell

$$
Q_O = q_O\, X
$$

- $q_O$ — specific oxygen uptake rate: oxygen required by one cell, in
  gram-mole per gram (dry cell weight) per second
  ($\text{gmol}/(\text{g} \cdot \text{s})$)
- $X$ — biomass (cell) concentration, in grams (dry cell weight) per
  cubic metre ($\text{g/m}^3$)
- $Q_O$ — oxygen uptake rate of the whole bioreactor (OUR), in
  gram-mole per cubic metre per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot
  \text{s})$)

### OTR equals OUR at steady state

At steady state, oxygen is consumed as fast as it is supplied, so the
oxygen transfer rate equals the oxygen uptake rate: $N_A = Q_O$, which
gives

$$
k_L a\, (C^*_{AL} - C_{AL}) = q_O\, X
$$

### Dependence on dissolved oxygen concentration

![Specific oxygen uptake rate plotted against dissolved oxygen concentration C_AL — the curve rises from the origin, climbs steeply, then flattens into a plateau past a critical concentration C_crit](/notes/bioprocess-eng/specific-oxygen-uptake-rate-vs-dissolved-oxygen.png)

- If $C_{AL} < C_{crit}$: there isn't enough dissolved oxygen around the
  cell, so it can't take up oxygen any faster than that supply allows.
- If $C_{AL} > C_{crit}$: the cell is already taking up oxygen as fast
  as it can, so more dissolved oxygen doesn't speed it up further.

### Two special conditions

**Maximum $k_L a$** — set $C_{AL} = C_{crit}$ in $N_A = Q_O$:

$$
k_{L}a_{max} = \frac{q_O\, X}{C^*_{AL} - C_{crit}}
$$

This is the minimum aeration/agitation capability a bioreactor must be
designed for to keep the culture above $C_{crit}$ at a given cell load.

**Maximum cell concentration** — set $C_{AL} = 0$ (no oxygen left in
the bioreactor) in $N_A = Q_O$:

$$
X_{max} = \frac{k_L a\, C^*_{AL}}{q_O}
$$

This gives the theoretical ceiling on biomass density a given aeration
setup can sustain — the limit you'd hit before scaling up oxygen
supply.

## Factors affecting $k_L a$

### 1. Bubble size

There's a reference bubble size around $2$–$3\ \text{mm}$:

- **Bubble size $> 2$–$3\ \text{mm}$**: interfacial area $a$ drops, so
  $k_L a$ decreases.
- **Bubble size $< 2$–$3\ \text{mm}$**: surface tension turns tiny
  bubbles into rigid spheres, which stops internal circulation and
  slightly lowers $k_L$ — but the resulting jump in interfacial area
  $a$ easily outweighs that drop, so $k_L a$ increases overall.

### 2. Viscosity

- **Viscosity↑** → resistance to OTR increases → $k_L a$ decreases.
- **Viscosity↓** → resistance to OTR decreases → $k_L a$ increases.

### 3. Stirrer speed

$k_L a$ increases directly with stirrer speed.

### 4. Temperature

Temperature pulls $k_L a$ in two directions at once:

- **Helps the kinetics:** temperature↑ → viscosity↓ and diffusivity
  $D_{AB}$↑ → $k_L$↑, so $k_L a$↑.
- **Hurts the driving force:** temperature↑ → gas solubility $C^*$↓
  (dissolution is exothermic), shrinking $(C^* - C)$ in

  $$
  N_A = k_L a\, (C^* - C)
  $$

So higher temperature speeds up transfer kinetics but weakens the
driving force behind it.

### 5. Gas sparging rate and coalescence

- **Sparging (superficial gas) velocity↑** → gas hold-up $\varepsilon_g$↑
  → $a$ (and $k_L a$)↑, since $a = 6\varepsilon_g/d_b$ — see "Gas hold-up"
  above.
- **Coalescence** → bubbles merge, $d_b$↑ → $a$↓ → $k_L a$↓. Coalescence
  always reduces interfacial area — it works against everything sparging
  and impeller shear are trying to achieve.

### 6. Antifoam agents

Antifoams (commonly silicone-based) control foam but come at a direct
cost to $k_L a$:

- They lower surface tension in a way that **promotes bubble
  coalescence** (bigger bubbles, lower $a$).
- They form a hydrophobic film at the gas-liquid interface that adds its
  own resistance to O$_2$ diffusion, lowering $k_L$ directly.

Excess antifoam is a common, easily overlooked way to cripple oxygen
transfer even while foam is well controlled — dose the minimum needed,
not the maximum tolerated.

#### Worked example: which factors decrease $k_L a$

**Question.** Which of the following factors will cause a DECREASE in
$k_L a$ in an agitated fermenter? (a) Addition of excess chemical
antifoam agents (b) Increase in broth apparent viscosity due to mycelial
fungal growth (c) Increasing the agitation speed from $200$ rpm to
$500$ rpm (d) Coalescence of smaller gas bubbles into larger bubbles.

**Solution.**

1. Excess antifoam promotes coalescence and adds its own interfacial
   resistance — both lower $k_L a$ — (a) decreases it.
2. Higher viscosity increases resistance to oxygen transfer, lowering
   $k_L a$ — (b) decreases it.
3. $k_L a$ increases directly with stirrer speed — raising rpm from
   $200$ to $500$ *increases* $k_L a$, it doesn't decrease it — (c) is
   not a decreasing factor.
4. Coalescence increases $d_b$, which lowers $a$ and therefore
   $k_L a$ — (d) decreases it.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (d) decrease $k_L a$; (c) increases it instead.

## Methods of measuring $k_L a$

### 1. Oxygen (gas) balance method

Measure the oxygen flow rate into and out of the fermenter, and back
out $N_A$ from the difference:

$$
N_A = \frac{1}{V_L}\Big[(F_g\, C_{AG})_{i} - (F_g\, C_{AG})_{o}\Big]
$$

- $V_L$ — volume of liquid in the fermenter, in cubic metres
  ($\text{m}^3$)
- $F_g$ — volumetric gas flow rate, in cubic metres per second
  ($\text{m}^3/\text{s}$)
- $C_{AG}$ — concentration of oxygen in the gas phase, in gram-mole per
  cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- subscripts $i$, $o$ — inlet and outlet gas streams

This measured $N_A$ is then equated to $N_A = k_L a\, (C^*_{AL} -
C_{AL})$ to solve for $k_L a$.

**Why it's useful:** this method is **non-invasive** — it samples the gas
streams, not the broth — and can run **continuously throughout an active
fermentation** without interrupting aeration or agitation, unlike the
dynamic gassing-out method below. It needs accurate measurement of both
inlet and outlet gas **flow rates and compositions** ($F_g$ and $C_{AG}$
on each side); it does *not* need any knowledge of liquid film thickness
— that's a film-theory construct behind $k_L$, not something this
macroscopic gas-phase balance ever measures directly.

**Simplifying the gas flow rates:** in general $F_g$ can differ between
inlet and outlet, because O$_2$ is consumed and CO$_2$ is produced at
different molar rates. If the **respiratory quotient**
($RQ = $ moles CO$_2$ produced $/$ moles O$_2$ consumed) is close to
$1$, the loss of O$_2$ moles is offset almost exactly by the gain of
CO$_2$ moles, so the total inlet and outlet volumetric gas flow rates end
up nearly equal ($F_{g,i} \approx F_{g,o}$) — simplifying the balance
considerably.

#### Worked example: what the off-gas balance method does and doesn't need

**Question.** Select the CORRECT statements regarding the off-gas oxygen
balancing method for measuring OTR: (a) It is a non-invasive, continuous
method that can be applied during active fermentation. (b) It requires
accurate measurement of inlet and exit gas flow rates and gas
compositions. (c) It depends heavily on knowing the exact value of the
liquid film thickness. (d) If respiratory quotient (RQ) is close to $1$,
inlet and outlet total volumetric gas flow rates are nearly equal.

**Solution.**

1. The method only samples gas streams, never the broth, and runs
   continuously without interrupting the process — (a) is correct.
2. The defining equation needs exactly $F_g$ and $C_{AG}$ on both the
   inlet and outlet sides — (b) is correct.
3. Film thickness is a film-theory construct behind $k_L$, not something
   this macroscopic gas-phase mass balance ever measures — (c) is false.
4. $RQ \approx 1$ means moles of O$_2$ consumed $\approx$ moles of CO$_2$
   produced, so the total gas-phase mole (and volumetric flow) count
   barely changes between inlet and outlet — (d) is correct.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (d) are correct; (c) is false.

### 2. Dynamic gassing-out method (most important)

Air supply to a respiring culture is cut off at $t_0$: with no more
oxygen coming in, $C_{AL}$ falls as the cells keep consuming it (the
OUR). Air is then switched back on, and $C_{AL}$ climbs back up toward
a steady level $\bar{C}_{AL}$. Two points on that recovery curve,
$(t_1, C_{AL_1})$ and $(t_2, C_{AL_2})$, give:

![Dissolved oxygen C_AL plotted against time: a flat line drops sharply once air flow is stopped at t0 as the culture consumes oxygen (OUR), bottoms out, then climbs back up through two marked recovery points (t1, C_AL1) and (t2, C_AL2) toward a steady reoxygenated level C̄_AL](/notes/bioprocess-eng/dynamic-gassing-out-method-curve.png)

$$
k_L a = \frac{\ln\left(\dfrac{\bar{C}_{AL} - C_{AL_1}}{\bar{C}_{AL} - C_{AL_2}}\right)}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

- $t_0$ — time at which the oxygen supply is cut off
- $t_1$, $t_2$ — two time points during reoxygenation, at which the
  instantaneous oxygen concentrations are $C_{AL_1}$ and $C_{AL_2}$
- $\bar{C}_{AL}$ — oxygen level reached at steady state after
  reoxygenation

($\ln$ is the natural log; if only base-10 log tables are available,
$\ln x = 2.303 \log x$.)

**De-oxygenation can be done two ways:** either simply cut off the air
supply and let respiring cells consume the dissolved O$_2$ naturally (as
above), or actively strip it by **sparging nitrogen gas** through the
broth — useful when respiration alone is too slow, or when testing
outside an active culture. Dissolved salts in the broth don't interfere
with either variant; the method works in real fermentation media exactly
as it does in plain water.

**Probe lag is a real limitation.** The method assumes the
dissolved-oxygen probe responds instantly to changes in $C_{AL}$, but
every DO probe has its own response time $\tau_p$ (set by its membrane
and internal electrode geometry). If $\tau_p$ is comparable to or larger
than $1/k_L a$, the probe can't keep up with the true recovery curve and
its reading lags behind reality — the raw data must then be corrected for
probe dynamics (e.g. fitting a first-order probe-response model) before
$k_L a$ can be extracted correctly. A fast probe ($\tau_p \ll 1/k_L a$)
avoids the correction entirely.

#### Worked example: correctness checks on the dynamic gassing-out method

**Question.** Regarding the dynamic gassing-out method for determining
$k_L a$ in a fermenter, which of the following statements is/are
CORRECT? (a) De-oxygenation of the broth is achieved by sparging nitrogen
gas or shutting off aeration while respiring cells consume oxygen.
(b) The slope of the line obtained by plotting
$\ln[(C^*-C_1)/(C^*-C_2)]$ against $(t_2-t_1)$ during re-aeration
directly yields $k_L a$. (c) If the DO probe response time ($\tau_p$) is
comparable to or larger than $1/k_L a$, probe lag must be corrected.
(d) The method cannot be used if the liquid broth contains dissolved
salts.

**Solution.**

1. Both de-oxygenation routes — cutting air, or sparging N$_2$ — are
   standard variants of this method — (a) is correct.
2. This is exactly the two-point form of the equation above: the ratio
   $\ln[(\bar{C}_{AL}-C_{AL_1})/(\bar{C}_{AL}-C_{AL_2})]$ over the elapsed
   time $(t_2-t_1)$ *is* the slope of $\ln(C^*-C_{AL})$ plotted against
   $t$ — (b) is correct.
3. A slow DO probe can't track the true recovery curve once its own
   response time approaches the process time scale $1/k_L a$, so its lag
   must be corrected for — (c) is correct.
4. Dissolved salts are present in essentially every real fermentation
   broth and don't interfere with this method — (d) is false.

**Answer:** (a), (b), and (c) are correct; (d) is false.

### 3. Sulphite oxidation method

Sodium sulphite reacts with dissolved oxygen as fast as it enters the
liquid, catalysed by trace amounts of **Cu$^{2+}$ or Co$^{2+}$** (added as
a copper or cobalt sulphate salt):

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + 0.5\, \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Co}^{2+}} \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4
$$

This only works because, at **moderate-to-high sulphite concentrations**
(above roughly $0.04\ \text{kmol/m}^3$), the reaction is **zero-order**
with respect to sulphite — the rate is independent of how much sulphite
is left, so it stays fast enough to consume oxygen the instant it
dissolves. That's what makes the method valid: the measured rate reflects
pure mass transfer, not reaction kinetics. (At low sulphite concentration
this breaks down — the reaction becomes rate-limiting instead, and the
method no longer measures $k_L a$ correctly.)

**Reaction order** describes how sensitive a reaction's rate is to a
reactant's concentration, from the empirical rate law
$\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^n$. It's the reason zero-order is a
requirement here, not just an incidental fact:

| Order ($n$) | Rate law | If sulphite were consumed during the assay | Effect on the method |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Zero** ($n=0$, actual) | rate $=k$ | Rate stays constant — sulphite level doesn't matter | Works: measured rate = pure O$_2$ mass transfer |
| **Half** ($n=0.5$) | rate $=k[\text{SO}_3^{2-}]^{0.5}$ | Rate drifts mildly as sulphite depletes | Partially confounded — some kinetic drift mixed into the reading |
| **First** ($n=1$) | rate $=k[\text{SO}_3^{2-}]$ | Rate falls proportionally with sulphite | Confounded — can't tell a rate change from less O$_2$ arriving vs. less sulphite left |
| **Second** ($n=2$) | rate $=k[\text{SO}_3^{2-}]^2$ | Rate falls off sharply (quadratically) | Worst case — small sulphite depletion swings the rate hard; kinetics dominate over mass transfer |

As $n$ rises, the measured oxidation rate stops being a clean proxy for
$k_L a$ and starts reflecting how much sulphite is left instead. The
method is only valid in the concentration range where the reaction has
settled into zero order — the one regime where the chemistry gets out of
the way and lets the measurement see the physics (mass transfer)
directly.

Because the sulphite binds oxygen immediately, no free oxygen
accumulates in solution — $C_{AL} = 0$ — so the driving force collapses
to just $C^*$:

$$
\text{OTR} = k_L a\, C^*
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
k_L a = \frac{\text{OTR}}{C^*}
$$

#### Worked example: catalyst and reaction order in the sulphite method

**Question.** During $k_L a$ measurement by the sulphite oxidation
method, sodium sulphite is converted to sodium sulphate in the presence
of a catalyst. The catalyst commonly used and the reaction order with
respect to sulphite at moderate concentrations are: (a) Cu$^{2+}$,
first-order (b) Co$^{2+}$, zero-order (c) Fe$^{2+}$, second-order
(d) Zn$^{2+}$, half-order.

**Solution.**

1. Co$^{2+}$ (cobaltous sulphate) is a standard catalyst for this
   reaction, alongside Cu$^{2+}$ — rules out (c) and (d), which use
   catalysts not associated with this method.
2. The method's validity depends on the reaction being fast enough to
   never be rate-limiting: at moderate-to-high sulphite concentrations,
   the rate is independent of sulphite concentration — zero-order.
3. (a) pairs a valid catalyst with the wrong order (first-order would
   make the reaction rate-limiting, defeating the method's purpose) — so
   only (b) pairs a valid catalyst with the correct kinetics.

**Answer: (b) Co$^{2+}$, zero-order.**

## Key points

- **Fick's law** ($J_A = -D_{AB}\, dC_A/dy$) always runs high concentration
  to low; **film theory** assumes a linear concentration profile across each
  stagnant film — steady-state diffusion, no accumulation, same reasoning as
  linear conduction through a wall.
- At the phase boundary itself there is no resistance — instantaneous
  equilibrium — and it's the **partition coefficient**, not diffusion, that
  decides the concentration jump between the two phases there.
- $k_L$ and $a$ move together and can't be measured separately, so they're
  lumped into $k_La$, the **volumetric mass transfer coefficient** — in
  aerobic fermentation this is the reactor's oxygen transfer capacity, the
  standard scale-up parameter.
- Of the eight resistances between a gas bubble and a cell's reaction site,
  diffusion through the **liquid film around the bubble** is the largest —
  which is why $k_La$ (built around that film) governs overall O$_2$
  transfer.
- At steady state, OTR = OUR: $k_La(C^*_{AL} - C_{AL}) = q_O X$. Setting
  $C_{AL} = C_{crit}$ gives the minimum $k_La$ a reactor needs to design
  for; setting $C_{AL} = 0$ gives the maximum biomass a given aeration setup
  can sustain.
- **Temperature is a trade-off for $k_La$**: it speeds up the kinetics
  (viscosity↓, diffusivity↑ → $k_L$↑) but shrinks the driving force
  (solubility $C^*$↓, since dissolution is exothermic).
- The **dynamic gassing-out method** is the standard way to measure $k_La$:
  cut the air supply, let dissolved O$_2$ fall, restart it, and read $k_La$
  off the slope of the recovery curve between two timepoints.
