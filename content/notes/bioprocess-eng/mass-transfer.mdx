## Mass transfer by diffusion

Mass transfer inside a bioreactor happens through diffusion.

### Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the thermal motion of fluid particles at any temperature
above absolute zero — 0 K, or −273 °C.

### Fick's law of diffusion

**Fick's law of diffusion** states that molecules move from areas of higher
concentration to areas of lower concentration.

![Dense cluster of component A particles at higher concentration on the left, with arrows passing through a circular cross-sectional area toward a sparser cluster of the same particles at lower concentration on the right](/notes/bioprocess-eng/fick-law-diffusion-flux-diagram.png)

Mass transfer of component A across a cross-sectional area perpendicular to
the direction of diffusion can be explained by this law.

$$
J_A = \frac{N_A}{a} = -D_{AB}\frac{dC_A}{dy}
$$

- $J_A$ — mass flux of component A, in kilograms per square metre-second
  ($\text{kg/(m}^2 \cdot \text{s)}$)
- $N_A$ — rate of mass transfer of component A, in kilograms per second
  ($\text{kg/s}$)
- $a$ — cross-sectional area, perpendicular to the direction of diffusion, in
  square metres ($\text{m}^2$) — not to be confused with the subscript $A$,
  which denotes the diffusing component
- $D_{AB}$ — diffusion coefficient of A through B, describing the ease of
  diffusion, in square metres per second ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
- $C_A$ — mass concentration of component A, in kilograms per cubic metre
  ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
- $y$ — distance in the direction of diffusion, in metres ($\text{m}$)

**The negative sign in the equation is because diffusion occurs from higher
concentration to lower concentration.**

## Film theory

**Film theory** states that at the interface between two phases, there
exists a thin, stagnant film or layer of fluid. Within this film, mass
transfer occurs only via molecular diffusion.

For example, when a bubble enters a bioreactor, a thin film of the same
bulk fluid surrounds the bubble. This film makes it difficult for gas
inside the bubble to diffuse out of the bubble and into the fluid.

### Concentration profile across the film

![Concentration profile of solute A across two phases in contact — organic phase bulk fluid on the left, aqueous phase bulk fluid on the right, separated by a phase boundary flanked by an organic film and an aqueous film; a curve shows concentration falling from CA1 in the aqueous bulk fluid, through CA1i and CA2i at the phase boundary, down to CA2 in the organic bulk fluid](/notes/bioprocess-eng/film-theory-concentration-profile-interface.png)

A solute moves from the bulk fluid on one side, across the phase boundary,
into the bulk fluid on the other side:

1. In the bulk fluid of the first phase (e.g. the aqueous phase), the
   solute is at concentration $C_{A1}$.
2. Moving through that phase's film toward the phase boundary, the
   concentration drops to $C_{A1i}$ (the interface concentration on this
   side) — the film poses resistance to transfer, since diffusion is the
   only way for the solute to move through it.
3. At the phase boundary itself, there is no transfer resistance —
   instantaneous thermodynamic equilibrium is established. The
   concentration can rise or drop here, from $C_{A1i}$ to $C_{A2i}$,
   because the solubility of the solute differs between the two phases —
   the partition coefficient decides the concentration on each side.
4. Moving across the second phase's film (e.g. the organic phase), the
   concentration continues to drop, from $C_{A2i}$ down to $C_{A2}$ in
   that phase's bulk fluid.

Overall: $C_{A1} > C_{A1i} > C_{A2i} > C_{A2}$.

Within each film, the concentration profile is **linear**. Film theory
assumes steady-state molecular diffusion through the film with no
accumulation, and integrating Fick's law under that assumption gives a
straight-line concentration gradient — the same reasoning that gives a
linear temperature profile across a wall in steady-state heat conduction.

### Rate of mass transfer (film theory)

Based on film theory, the mass transfer rate is:

$$
N_A = k_L\, a\, (C^* - C)
$$

- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of mass transfer, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $k_L$ — (liquid) film mass-transfer coefficient, in metres per second
  ($\text{m/s}$)
- $a$ — interfacial area per unit volume, in square metres per cubic
  metre ($\text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$)
- $C^*$ — saturation concentration: the maximum concentration of the
  solute that can dissolve in the liquid, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C$ — bulk concentration: the current concentration of the solute in the
  liquid, in gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

$k_L$ is how fast the molecule crosses the film: the thinner the film, the
higher $k_L$, and the faster the transfer.

$C^* - C$ is the driving force for mass transfer: the bigger the
difference between the saturation concentration and the bulk
concentration, the more strongly the system pulls the solute into the
liquid.

### Interfacial area per unit volume ($a$)

**Interfacial area per unit volume** ($a$) is the total interfacial area
between the two phases, divided by the total volume of the liquid (the
bulk liquid) in the vessel — not the total vessel volume including the gas
phase. In practice, the gas phase (bubbles) occupies only a small fraction
of the vessel, so the liquid volume is used as the "total volume" in this
ratio.

For a sphere, $a = S/V = 6/d$ — so smaller bubbles mean higher $a$. Splitting
a fixed total gas volume into $n$ equal bubbles multiplies $a$ (and the
total interfacial area) by $n^{1/3}$: a single $10\ \text{mm}$ bubble gives
$a = 600\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$, while 100 smaller bubbles of the same
total volume give $a \approx 2790\ \text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$. This is why
sparging aims to break gas into fine bubbles rather than large ones.

### Volumetric mass transfer coefficient ($k_L a$)

$k_L$ and $a$ are hard to measure separately, so they are lumped into a
single **volumetric mass transfer coefficient**:

$$
N_A = k_L a\, (C^* - C)
$$

- $k_L a$ — volumetric mass transfer coefficient, in reciprocal seconds
  ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of mass transfer, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $C^*$ — saturation concentration, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C$ — bulk concentration, in gram-mole per cubic metre
  ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

$k_L$ and $a$ change together (e.g. faster impeller speed raises both),
so they're rarely separated in practice. In aerobic fermentation, $A$
is oxygen, so $k_L a$ represents the bioreactor's **oxygen transfer
capacity** — the standard parameter for bioreactor design and scale-up.

## Oxygen uptake in a bioreactor

### Rate of oxygen transfer

$$
N_A = k_L a\, (C^*_{AL} - C_{AL})
$$

- $N_A$ — volumetric rate of oxygen transfer (OTR), in gram-mole per
  cubic metre per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot \text{s})$)
- $k_L a$ — volumetric mass transfer coefficient, in reciprocal seconds
  ($\text{s}^{-1}$)
- $C^*_{AL}$ — saturation concentration of oxygen in the liquid (its
  maximum solubility), in gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- $C_{AL}$ — instantaneous concentration of oxygen in the liquid, in
  gram-mole per cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)

![Path of an oxygen molecule from a gas bubble to a cell's reaction site, numbered in eight steps: (1) interior of the bubble to the gas-liquid interface, (2) across the gas-liquid interface, (3) through the liquid film around the bubble, (4) across the bulk liquid, (5) through the liquid film around the cell, (6) across the liquid-cell interface, (7) to an individual cell within a clump, (8) through the cytoplasm to the reaction site — with k_L a labeling the overall path](/notes/bioprocess-eng/oxygen-transfer-resistance-steps.png)

Oxygen travels from a gas bubble to a cell's reaction site through eight
resistances in series:

1. Transfer from the interior of the bubble to the gas-liquid interface
2. Transfer across the gas-liquid interface
3. Diffusion through the liquid film around the bubble
4. Transport through the bulk liquid
5. Diffusion through the liquid film around the cell
6. Movement across the liquid-cell interface
7. For cells in a clump, diffusion to the individual cell
8. Transport through the cytoplasm to the reaction site

**Step 3 — diffusion through the liquid film around the bubble — is the
largest resistance**, which is why $k_L a$ (built around that film) is
the parameter that governs overall oxygen transfer.

### Factors affecting cellular oxygen demand

1. Species of bacteria consuming the oxygen
2. Growth phase the cells are in
3. Nature of the carbon source fed to the cells

### Rate of oxygen consumption per cell

$$
Q_O = q_O\, X
$$

- $q_O$ — specific oxygen uptake rate: oxygen required by one cell, in
  gram-mole per gram (dry cell weight) per second
  ($\text{gmol}/(\text{g} \cdot \text{s})$)
- $X$ — biomass (cell) concentration, in grams (dry cell weight) per
  cubic metre ($\text{g/m}^3$)
- $Q_O$ — oxygen uptake rate of the whole bioreactor (OUR), in
  gram-mole per cubic metre per second ($\text{gmol}/(\text{m}^3 \cdot
  \text{s})$)

### OTR equals OUR at steady state

At steady state, oxygen is consumed as fast as it is supplied, so the
oxygen transfer rate equals the oxygen uptake rate: $N_A = Q_O$, which
gives

$$
k_L a\, (C^* - C) = q_O\, X
$$

### Dependence on dissolved oxygen concentration

![Specific oxygen uptake rate plotted against dissolved oxygen concentration C_AL — the curve rises from the origin, climbs steeply, then flattens into a plateau past a critical concentration C_crit](/notes/bioprocess-eng/specific-oxygen-uptake-rate-vs-dissolved-oxygen.png)

- If $C_{AL} < C_{crit}$: there isn't enough dissolved oxygen around the
  cell, so it can't take up oxygen any faster than that supply allows.
- If $C_{AL} > C_{crit}$: the cell is already taking up oxygen as fast
  as it can, so more dissolved oxygen doesn't speed it up further.

### Two special conditions

**Maximum $k_L a$** — set $C_{AL} = C_{crit}$ in $N_A = Q_O$:

$$
k_{L}a_{max} = \frac{q_O\, X}{C^*_{AL} - C_{crit}}
$$

This is the minimum aeration/agitation capability a bioreactor must be
designed for to keep the culture above $C_{crit}$ at a given cell load.

**Maximum cell concentration** — set $C_{AL} = 0$ (no oxygen left in
the bioreactor) in $N_A = Q_O$:

$$
X_{max} = \frac{k_L a\, C^*_{AL}}{q_O}
$$

This gives the theoretical ceiling on biomass density a given aeration
setup can sustain — the limit you'd hit before scaling up oxygen
supply.

## Factors affecting $k_L a$

### 1. Bubble size

There's a reference bubble size around $2$–$3\ \text{mm}$:

- **Bubble size $> 2$–$3\ \text{mm}$**: interfacial area $a$ drops, so
  $k_L a$ decreases.
- **Bubble size $< 2$–$3\ \text{mm}$**: surface tension turns tiny
  bubbles into rigid spheres, which stops internal circulation and
  slightly lowers $k_L$ — but the resulting jump in interfacial area
  $a$ easily outweighs that drop, so $k_L a$ increases overall.

### 2. Viscosity

- **Viscosity↑** → resistance to OTR increases → $k_L a$ decreases.
- **Viscosity↓** → resistance to OTR decreases → $k_L a$ increases.

### 3. Stirrer speed

$k_L a$ increases directly with stirrer speed.

### 4. Temperature

Temperature pulls $k_L a$ in two directions at once:

- **Helps the kinetics:** temperature↑ → viscosity↓ and diffusivity
  $D_{AB}$↑ → $k_L$↑, so $k_L a$↑.
- **Hurts the driving force:** temperature↑ → gas solubility $C^*$↓
  (dissolution is exothermic), shrinking $(C^* - C)$ in

  $$
  N_A = k_L a\, (C^* - C)
  $$

So higher temperature speeds up transfer kinetics but weakens the
driving force behind it.

## Methods of measuring $k_L a$

### 1. Oxygen (gas) balance method

Measure the oxygen flow rate into and out of the fermenter, and back
out $N_A$ from the difference:

$$
N_A = \frac{1}{V_L}\Big[(F_g\, C_{AG})_{i} - (F_g\, C_{AG})_{o}\Big]
$$

- $V_L$ — volume of liquid in the fermenter, in cubic metres
  ($\text{m}^3$)
- $F_g$ — volumetric gas flow rate, in cubic metres per second
  ($\text{m}^3/\text{s}$)
- $C_{AG}$ — concentration of oxygen in the gas phase, in gram-mole per
  cubic metre ($\text{gmol/m}^3$)
- subscripts $i$, $o$ — inlet and outlet gas streams

This measured $N_A$ is then equated to $N_A = k_L a\, (C^*_{AL} -
C_{AL})$ to solve for $k_L a$.

### 2. Dynamic gassing-out method (most important)

Air supply to a respiring culture is cut off at $t_0$: with no more
oxygen coming in, $C_{AL}$ falls as the cells keep consuming it (the
OUR). Air is then switched back on, and $C_{AL}$ climbs back up toward
a steady level $\bar{C}_{AL}$. Two points on that recovery curve,
$(t_1, C_{AL_1})$ and $(t_2, C_{AL_2})$, give:

![Dissolved oxygen C_AL plotted against time: a flat line drops sharply once air flow is stopped at t0 as the culture consumes oxygen (OUR), bottoms out, then climbs back up through two marked recovery points (t1, C_AL1) and (t2, C_AL2) toward a steady reoxygenated level C̄_AL](/notes/bioprocess-eng/dynamic-gassing-out-method-curve.png)

$$
k_L a = \frac{\ln\left(\dfrac{\bar{C}_{AL} - C_{AL_1}}{\bar{C}_{AL} - C_{AL_2}}\right)}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

- $t_0$ — time at which the oxygen supply is cut off
- $t_1$, $t_2$ — two time points during reoxygenation, at which the
  instantaneous oxygen concentrations are $C_{AL_1}$ and $C_{AL_2}$
- $\bar{C}_{AL}$ — oxygen level reached at steady state after
  reoxygenation

($\ln$ is the natural log; if only base-10 log tables are available,
$\ln x = 2.303 \log x$.)

### 3. Sulphite oxidation method

Sodium sulphite reacts with dissolved oxygen as fast as it enters the
liquid:

$$
\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + 0.5\, \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4
$$

Because the sulphite binds oxygen immediately, no free oxygen
accumulates in solution — $C_{AL} = 0$ — so the driving force collapses
to just $C^*$:

$$
\text{OTR} = k_L a\, C^*
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
k_L a = \frac{\text{OTR}}{C^*}
$$
