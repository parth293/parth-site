This is the canonical version. It changes as my daughter grows and as I learn
what actually worked, but at any point there is exactly one of these — new
thinking goes in a dated post that argues for a change, and once I've made the
change, this page is what gets updated. See
[why fifteen, and how this list works](/notes/life-eng/why-fifteen) for the
reasoning behind the structure itself.

**Target: functionally independent by fifteen.** Not eighteen. The legal age
of adulthood is a compliance date, not a competence date, and I'd rather
under-shoot and keep adding than plan to a deadline that lets everyone relax
for the last three years.

**Principles and skills, not facts.** Nothing on this list is knowledge for
its own sake. Every entry is something she can act on, and every entry should
translate into a real decision she'll actually face — what to eat, how to
treat someone who's wronged her, whether to pick up a cigarette. What she gets
curious about beyond this is hers to choose; I'm not assigning trivia.

## 0–4

- **The golden rule, modelled before it's explained.** Treat others as you
  would want to be treated. At this age it isn't a lesson, it's what she sees
  me do when I'm frustrated, tired, or dealing with someone who's rude to me.
- **A daily reading habit**, even before she's reading herself — being read to
  is what builds the appetite the skill later runs on.

## 5–8

- **Independent reading fluency.** Not a specific book list — the bare-minimum
  muscle so that whatever she wants to go deep on later, self-taught, is
  available to her. This is the one skill that unlocks every other skill.
- **Times tables to 100.** Arithmetic fast enough to be invisible, so that
  later math is limited by the new idea, not by recomputing 7×8 every time it
  shows up.
- **The golden rule, explained.** Why it works, not just that it's the rule —
  the mechanism (people mostly treat you the way you treat them, over enough
  time) rather than the platitude.

## 9–11

- **Basic cooking.** Enough to feed herself a real meal without help — not
  recipes memorised, but the underlying logic of why a dish works, so she can
  improvise.
- **Basic nutrition.** What a diet needs and why, so food choices later are
  informed rather than inherited or accidental.
- **How to learn, mechanistically: spaced repetition and neuroplasticity.**
  That forgetting on a schedule and re-testing yourself right before you'd
  forget builds stronger recall than re-reading, and why — the brain
  strengthens connections it's forced to retrieve, not ones it passively
  re-sees. Taught here rather than later because it changes how she does every
  subject from this point forward, not just as a fact she owns.

## 12–15

- **Fitness as a habit, not an event.** Why regular movement matters, before
  the version of her that has to decide this for herself without a parent
  reminding her shows up.
- **Basic anatomy — the parts that fail first.** Muscles, tendons, ligaments,
  and specifically posture, because bad posture compounds quietly for decades
  before it becomes a real problem, and the earlier the habit is right the
  cheaper it is to keep right.
- **Why the common bad habits are bad, mechanistically.** Smoking, drinking —
  not "these are bad for you" as an assertion, but what they actually do,
  stated plainly enough that avoiding them is a reasoned decision and not just
  obedience to a rule she'll drop the moment no one's watching.
- **Measuring and improving her own heart health.** What the basic numbers are
  (resting heart rate, blood pressure) and what actually moves them, so
  cardiovascular fitness is something she tracks and acts on rather than a
  thing that's fine until a doctor tells her otherwise at forty.
- **Measuring and improving her own lung health.** Same idea for breathing
  capacity and endurance — what it is, roughly how you'd notice it's declining,
  and what training actually improves it.
- **Flexibility and range of motion as a maintained thing, not a fixed trait.**
  That mobility is lost by default through disuse and kept through
  maintenance, same mechanism as the posture item above but for joints
  generally.
- **Division of labour, and specialisation as the source of higher
  productivity.** Why one person doing one thing repeatedly and trading for
  everything else beats everyone doing everything for themselves — the
  mechanism behind why she'll specialise in something rather than try to be
  self-sufficient, and why trade isn't a zero-sum transaction.
- **Supply and demand as the thing that sets prices, not a fixed rule.** That
  a price is where two curves cross, not an authority's decision, so that
  scarcity, gluts, and prices moving both ways make sense as the same
  mechanism rather than separate, confusing events.
- **Money: the asset classes available to a regular person, and their
  tradeoffs.** What an asset actually is (something that can generate income
  or appreciate, as distinct from a liability that just costs money to hold),
  and a working comparison of the main options she'll actually have access to
  — equities, gold, real estate, cash and cash-equivalents, each with its own
  liquidity, volatility, and behaviour under inflation.
- **A share of stock is ownership in a business, not a ticker that moves.**
  The mechanism behind index funds versus actively managed mutual funds versus
  picking individual stocks — what each actually is, why costs compound
  against you over decades, and why "beating the market by picking stocks" is
  a much harder game than it looks from outside it.
- **Gold as an inflation hedge — and what inflation actually is.** Why
  governments print money, what that does to the value of cash sitting still,
  and why gold's specific property (it can't be printed) is what makes it a
  hedge rather than just another commodity.
- **The case for becoming an asset owner early, and keeping free cash low.**
  Why compounding rewards time in the market more than timing it, and why
  idle cash is a cost (inflation erodes it) not a safe default — this is the
  synthesis of the items above into an actual decision: what to do with money
  as soon as she has any.
- **Paul Graham's hierarchy of disagreement, and how to argue well.** The
  difference between refuting the central point and refuting tone, name-calling,
  or a weak version of the argument — so that disagreeing with someone is a
  skill aimed at being right, not at winning.
- **Empathy as a set of learnable techniques, not just a trait.** Concretely:
  the negotiation and listening techniques from *Never Split the Difference*
  (Chris Voss) — labeling emotions, mirroring, calibrated questions — as tools
  for understanding what someone actually wants, not manipulation tricks.
- **Basic social skills: how to be genuinely likable.** *How to Win Friends
  and Influence People* (Dale Carnegie) as the starting text — the mechanism
  being that people respond to genuine interest and being made to feel
  important, not to charisma as an innate trait.
- **Happiness tracks the gap between expectation and reality, not the reality
  alone.** That deliberately keeping expectations calibrated (not lowered as
  pessimism, but set accurately) is one of the few levers she'll have direct
  control over, versus circumstances she often won't — a mechanism, not a
  platitude about gratitude.

---

**Learning milestones and books.** Each item above should eventually be tied
to the specific book, source, or teaching moment that landed it — not just the
milestone age. I've started that for the communication cluster (Paul Graham's
disagreement hierarchy, *Never Split the Difference*, *How to Win Friends and
Influence People*); the rest of the list still needs its sources attached. More
books to come as I identify them.

**Open, not yet placed:** items I know belong somewhere on this list but
haven't decided which milestone they belong to — how to recognise when someone
is taking advantage of her, basic self-defence, how to disagree with someone
without ending the relationship (distinct from arguing well — this is about
repair afterward).
