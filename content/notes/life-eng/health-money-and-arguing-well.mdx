The [master list](/notes/life-eng/master-list) had a gap I kept noticing every
time I re-read it: the 12–15 block covered fitness as a habit and the
mechanics of common bad habits, but nothing about *measuring* health, nothing
about money, and nothing about how to disagree with someone without either
folding or turning it into a fight. All three belong in the same window she
already occupies for the rest of that block, so I added them together rather
than as three separate edits.

## Health as something you measure, not something you feel

"Fitness as a habit" was already on the list, but it's a vague enough target
that it's easy to satisfy by feeling fine. I added three more specific items:
measuring and improving heart health, measuring and improving lung health, and
treating flexibility as a maintained thing rather than a fixed trait. The
mechanism behind all three is the same one that's already on the list for
posture — decline through disuse is quiet and compounds for decades before it
becomes a visible problem, and by the time it's visible it's expensive to
reverse. Giving her the actual numbers to track (resting heart rate, blood
pressure, roughly how you'd notice lung capacity declining) turns "stay
healthy" from a platitude into something she can act on, which is the bar the
whole list is held to.

## Money: asset ownership as the central idea

This is the biggest single addition, and it took the most thinking to place.
I didn't want a list of financial products — I wanted the one idea that makes
the rest of personal finance make sense, which is that an asset is something
that works for you (generates income or appreciates) and a liability is
something that costs you money to hold, and most people never make that
distinction explicit.

From there the list builds outward: what asset classes a regular person
actually has access to (equities, gold, real estate, cash) and their
tradeoffs; that a share of stock is ownership in a business, which is the
fact that makes index funds, mutual funds, and stock-picking a coherent
comparison instead of three unrelated financial products; why inflation
exists (governments printing money) and what it does to cash sitting still;
gold specifically as a hedge because it can't be printed, which is a
different property than "gold is valuable"; and finally the synthesis — that
compounding rewards time in the market, which means becoming an asset owner
early beats trying to time it, and that idle free cash is a cost, not a safe
default, because inflation is eroding it while it sits.

I placed all of this at 12–15 rather than splitting it earlier. Money
concepts before that age tend to be either too abstract (she has no income or
expenses to apply them to) or reduced to "save your allowance," which teaches
the wrong lesson — saving cash is not the goal, becoming an asset owner is.

## Communication as technique, not personality

The third cluster is arguing and connecting with people, and I'm treating both
as learnable skills with named sources rather than traits she either has or
doesn't. Paul Graham's hierarchy of disagreement gives her a concrete way to
tell the difference between refuting someone's actual point and refuting their
tone or a weaker version of their argument — most adults never make this
distinction and it shows in how badly they argue. *Never Split the Difference*
gives specific techniques (labeling, mirroring, calibrated questions) for
understanding what someone wants, which I'm filing under empathy because
that's what the techniques are actually for, not manipulation. *How to Win
Friends and Influence People* is the starting text for the broader "how to be
likable" question, on the theory that likability is mostly genuine interest in
other people rather than an innate trait.

I expect to add more books to this cluster specifically — it's the one where
a single well-chosen text does more work than a paragraph of my own
explanation, and I've only listed the ones I'm certain about so far.

## How to learn, placed earlier than the rest

Spaced repetition and the basic idea of neuroplasticity went into 9–11 rather
than 12–15, and that's a deliberate break from grouping these additions
together by age. The reasoning: this isn't a fact about the world she carries
around, it's a method that changes how she does everything else on the list
from the point she has it. Forced retrieval on a schedule builds stronger
recall than re-reading — the brain strengthens the connections it has to work
to retrieve, not the ones it passively re-sees. Every other item on this list
that involves remembering something benefits from her having this tool
early, so it goes in as early as the reading-fluency window closes and she
has real study material to apply it to.

## Economics: division of labour and supply and demand

These sit next to the money cluster because they're the mechanism underneath
it, not because they're about money specifically. Division of labour and
specialisation is why trade isn't zero-sum — one person doing one thing
repeatedly and trading for everything else outproduces everyone trying to be
self-sufficient, which is also the reason she'll eventually specialise in
something for a living rather than generalise. Supply and demand is the
companion idea: that a price is where two curves cross, not a number an
authority sets, so that a shortage, a glut, and an ordinary price change are
all the same mechanism instead of three separate confusing events. Both are
prerequisites for the money cluster actually making sense rather than being
memorised as rules.

## Happiness as a gap, not a state

The last addition is smaller but I wanted it on the list rather than assumed:
that happiness tracks the gap between expectation and reality more than it
tracks reality on its own, and that calibrating expectations accurately (not
lowering them as pessimism) is one of the few levers she'll have direct
control over. I filed this under 12–15 because it only makes sense once she's
old enough to have expectations worth examining — a toddler doesn't have the
gap yet.

## What's still open

Money and communication both raise the same question the [original
reasoning post](/notes/life-eng/why-fifteen) flagged as provisional: I'm
placing all of this at 12–15 on instinct, not because I've watched a child
actually absorb asset-ownership concepts at that age. It may split once she's
there — the mechanical parts of money (what an asset is, why inflation
happens) probably land earlier than the judgment parts (when to buy, how much
risk to take). I'll adjust when I have evidence instead of a guess.
