My daughter is six months old, which means I have roughly nothing to go on
yet about who she'll turn out to be. What I do have is a belief I've held for
longer than I've been a parent: that most people are never deliberately
taught the principles and skills they end up running their adult life on.
They pick them up by accident, late, usually from a bad experience that a
five-minute conversation years earlier would have prevented. I don't want
that to be how my daughter learns to eat well, or to spot someone treating
her badly, or to have a habit that keeps her spine straight at fifty.

So this is the [master list](/notes/life-eng/master-list) — the working
curriculum, and the reasoning behind how I built it.

## Why fifteen, not eighteen

Eighteen is a legal line, not a developmental one. If I plan the curriculum
to finish at eighteen, the natural failure mode is that everything slides
right — there's always another three years, until there isn't. Targeting
fifteen means I'm forced to have the harder conversations earlier, and it
leaves three years of margin if something doesn't land the first time. I'm
treating fifteen as conservative on purpose: better to finish early and keep
reinforcing than to discover at seventeen that something important never got
covered.

## Why principles and skills, not knowledge

I'm not building a list of things she should know. I'm building a list of
things she should be able to *do* — cook a meal, read a book she chose
herself, tell when a habit is quietly damaging her, treat someone decently
under pressure. What she gets curious about beyond that is entirely hers to
choose, and I don't think it's my job to pick it for her. A parent who
decides their kid must know a specific set of facts is optimising for the
parent's comfort, not the kid's independence. A parent who never teaches
anything deliberately is optimising for nothing at all. The list is the
attempt to hold the line between those two failure modes: teach the
transferable thing, stay out of the way on everything else.

## Why age milestones, and why they're provisional

Grouping by age (0–4, 5–8, 9–11, 12–15) instead of by subject is deliberate —
the question I want the list to answer at any point is "what does she need
*now*," not "what does the reading section eventually contain." But I want to
be honest about how much guessing is in the current placement. Some items —
reading fluency at five to eight, arithmetic fluency in the same window — are
placed with real confidence; they track how most children actually develop.
Others — exactly when to have the posture-and-bad-habits conversation, for
instance — are placed on instinct and will probably move once I've watched
her actually get there.

## Why one master list instead of a running series

The list itself stays singular and current — there is one canonical version,
and it gets edited in place, the way a spec gets edited rather than
re-published. But *why* it changed is worth keeping. So the mechanism is:
edits happen quietly on the master list; anything that changed for a real
reason — a milestone that turned out to be wrong, an item I initially missed,
something I read or watched happen that changed my mind — gets its own dated
post like this one, arguing for the change. The list tells you what I
currently believe. The posts tell you why I used to believe something
different.

This is the first post in that series. There will be more as she grows and
the list turns out to be wrong in places I can't yet predict.
