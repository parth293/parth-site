## Atomic number and mass number

- **Atomic number (Z)** — proton count. Identifies the element; fixes its
  position on the periodic table.
- **Mass number (A)** — protons + neutrons. Electrons are ignored
  (negligible mass).

### Isotopes of hydrogen — same Z, different A

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Protium (¹H) | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Deuterium (²H, D) | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Tritium (³H, T) | 1 | 2 | 3 |

## Water — structure and polarity

- **Molecular weight** — 18 daltons (2 from its two H atoms + 16 from O).
- **Bond angle** — H–O–H is 104.5°.
- Bent shape + oxygen's higher electronegativity → uneven charge
  distribution → water is **polar**.
- **CO₂ is not polar** — linear and symmetrical, so its two C=O bond
  dipoles point opposite and cancel exactly.

### Why its properties look "too high" for its size

- Unusually high melting point, boiling point, heat of vaporization, and
  surface tension for an 18-dalton molecule.
- Cause: **intermolecular hydrogen bonding** between water molecules —
  breaking enough of these bonds to melt, boil, or vaporize it takes far
  more energy than the low molecular weight alone would suggest.

## Water as a solvent

- Water is the **universal solvent** — being polar, it dissolves most
  polar and ionic compounds.
- **Hydrophilic** — polar; dissolves in water.
- **Hydrophobic** — nonpolar; virtually insoluble in water.
- **Amphipathic** compounds have both a polar and a nonpolar region in the
  same molecule — the hydrophilic region interacts with water, the
  hydrophobic region avoids it.
- **Dielectric constant** — a solvent's capacity to neutralize the
  attraction between opposite electrical charges.
  - Water has a high dielectric constant → weakens the attraction between
    oppositely charged ions (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻) → makes water a powerful
    solvent for salts.

## Water — acid/base behavior

- **Amphoteric** — a substance that can act as both an acid and a base.
- **Amphiprotic** — a substance that can either donate or accept a proton
  (H⁺). Water is both amphoteric and amphiprotic.

### Ionization of water

$$
H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-
$$

- $H_2O$ — water
- $H^+$ — hydrogen ion (proton)
- $OH^-$ — hydroxide ion

### Autoionization of water

$$
2H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-
$$

- $H_3O^+$ — hydronium ion, water's conjugate acid
- $OH^-$ — hydroxide ion, water's conjugate base

- Degree of ionization is measured by **electrical conductivity**.
- In pure water, $[H^+] = 10^{-7}$ mol/L — i.e. 1 L of pure water contains
  $10^{-7}$ mol of H⁺ ions.
- On electrolysis, $H_3O^+$ (cation) migrates to the **cathode**; $OH^-$
  (anion) migrates to the **anode**.

### Equilibrium constant of water's ionization

$$
K_{eq} = \dfrac{[H^+][OH^-]}{[H_2O]}
$$

- $K_{eq}$ — equilibrium constant of water's ionization, mol/L
- $[H^+]$ — hydrogen ion concentration, mol/L
- $[OH^-]$ — hydroxide ion concentration, mol/L
- $[H_2O]$ — molar concentration of water, mol/L

- Water's degree of autoionization is very small, so $[H_2O]$ is treated as
  constant: $55.5$ mol/L.
- $K_{eq}$ at 25°C = $1.8 \times 10^{-16}$ mol/L.

### Ion product of water ($K_w$)

$$
K_w = [H^+][OH^-]
$$

- $K_w$ — ion product of water: product of $[H^+]$ and $[OH^-]$ in pure
  water, mol²/L²
- $[H^+]$ — hydrogen ion concentration, mol/L
- $[OH^-]$ — hydroxide ion concentration, mol/L

- $K_w = K_{eq} \times 55.5$ (rolling $[H_2O]$'s constant concentration into
  $K_{eq}$).
- $K_w$ at 25°C = $10^{-14}$ mol²/L².

### Acidic, basic, and neutral solutions

| Solution | $[H^+]$ / $[H_3O^+]$ |
| --- | --- |
| Acidic | $> 10^{-7}$ M |
| Neutral | $= 10^{-7}$ M |
| Basic | $< 10^{-7}$ M |

### pH

$$
pH = -\log[H^+]
$$

- $pH$ — measure of acidity, unitless
- $[H^+]$ — hydrogen ion concentration, mol/L

## Acids, Bases and pH

- **Brønsted-Lowry acid** — a substance that donates a proton (H⁺).
- **Brønsted-Lowry base** — a substance that accepts a proton (H⁺).

### Ionization of HCl in water

$$
\underbrace{HCl}_{\text{acid}_1} + \underbrace{H_2O}_{\text{base}_2} \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{Cl^-}_{\text{base}_1} + \underbrace{H_3O^+}_{\text{acid}_2}
$$

- $HCl$ — acid 1: donates a proton to become its conjugate base, $Cl^-$
- $Cl^-$ — conjugate base of $HCl$
- $H_2O$ — base 2: accepts a proton to become its conjugate acid, $H_3O^+$
- $H_3O^+$ — conjugate acid of $H_2O$

Conjugate acid-base pairs: $HCl / Cl^-$ and $H_3O^+ / H_2O$.

- **Strong acids** ionize completely in aqueous solution.
- **Weak acids** do not ionize completely — some HA remains undissociated
  at equilibrium.
- **Equilibrium** — rate of the forward reaction equals rate of the
  reverse reaction; no net change in concentration.

### Acid dissociation constant ($K_a$)

$$
HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-
$$

$$
K_a = \dfrac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}
$$

- $HA$ — undissociated acid
- $H^+$ — hydrogen ion (proton)
- $A^-$ — conjugate base of $HA$
- $K_a$ — acid dissociation constant (also acid ionization constant, or
  acidity constant), mol/L

$K_a$ measures an acid's tendency to lose a proton and form its conjugate
base — the equilibrium constant of that ionization.

### $pK_a$

$$
pK_a = -\log K_a
$$

- $pK_a$ — negative log of $K_a$, unitless
- $K_a$ — acid dissociation constant, mol/L

$pK_a$ is the standard way weak acids' proton-donating strength is
reported. The stronger the tendency to dissociate a proton, the larger
$K_a$ and the **lower** $pK_a$.

| Acid | Type | $K_a$ (mol/L) | $pK_a$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HCl | Strong | $\sim 10^7$ | $\sim -7$ |
| Acetic acid ($CH_3COOH$) | Weak | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | $4.76$ |

A strong acid like $HCl$ ionizes essentially completely, so $K_a$ is huge
and $pK_a$ is a large negative number. A weak acid like acetic acid only
partly ionizes, so $K_a$ is small and $pK_a$ is a small positive number.

### pH and $pK_a$ — the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

$$
pH = pK_a + \log \dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA]}
$$

- $pH$ — measure of acidity of the solution, unitless
- $pK_a$ — acid dissociation constant of the acid in question, unitless
- $[A^-]$ — conjugate base concentration, mol/L
- $[HA]$ — undissociated acid concentration, mol/L

Relates a buffered solution's pH to its $pK_a$ and the ratio of
conjugate base to undissociated acid present.

- When $[A^-] = [HA]$, the ratio is $1$ and $\log(1) = 0$, so
  $pH = pK_a$.
- **Physical significance** — at $pH = pK_a$, the acid is exactly 50%
  dissociated: equal concentrations of $HA$ and $A^-$. This is the
  buffer's midpoint, where it has the greatest capacity to resist a pH
  change from added acid or base.

## Titration

- **Titration** — a technique for determining the concentration of a
  solution of unknown concentration by reacting it with a solution of
  known concentration, added incrementally until the reaction is
  complete.
- **Titrant** — the solution of known concentration, added incrementally
  (e.g. from a burette).
- **Analyte** — the solution of unknown concentration being determined.

### Titrating a weak acid with a strong base

1. The titrant (a strong base, e.g. $NaOH$) is added to the acid (the
   analyte) in small increments.
2. Addition continues until the acid is fully neutralized.
3. The point of neutralization is determined with an indicator dye
   (color change) or a pH meter.

### Titration curve

- **Titration curve** — a plot of the solution's pH (y-axis) against the
  amount of $OH^-$ (titrant) added (x-axis).

![Titration curve of acetic acid: pH (0–9) plotted against OH⁻ equivalents added (0–1). The curve starts near pH 2–3, rises, then flattens into a buffering plateau centered at 0.5 equivalents where pH = pKa = 4.76 (buffering region pH 3.76 to 5.76, shaded). It then rises steeply again approaching 1.0 equivalents — the equivalence point and the curve's inflection point — before leveling off near pH 7.5–8, where the solution is mostly CH₃COO⁻.](/notes/biotech-eng/titration-curve-acetic-acid.png)

### Sequence of the curve, in order as $OH^-$ is added

Picture a beaker of acetic acid, and you're dripping $NaOH$ in slowly.

1. **Start** (0 equivalents) — the beaker holds almost nothing but
   intact $CH_3COOH$ molecules; only a tiny fraction have broken apart
   into $H^+$ and $CH_3COO^-$. Lots of free $H^+$ around, so pH is low.
2. **Early rise** — you start adding drops of $OH^-$. Each one grabs a
   free $H^+$ and becomes water. There's barely any $CH_3COO^-$ around
   yet to cushion this, so the free-$H^+$ count drops fast and pH
   climbs quickly.
3. **Buffering zone begins** — enough $CH_3COOH$ has now turned into
   $CH_3COO^-$ that both are floating around in similar amounts. From
   here, each drop of $OH^-$ reacts with a $CH_3COOH$ molecule instead
   of depleting free $H^+$ directly — and there's so much $CH_3COOH$ in
   reserve that this barely moves the pH. This is the flat stretch on
   the graph (roughly $pK_a \pm 1$; for acetic acid, pH $3.76$ to
   $5.76$).
4. **Half-equivalence point** (0.5 equivalents) — exactly half the
   original $CH_3COOH$ has now been converted, so $CH_3COOH$ and
   $CH_3COO^-$ are sitting in the beaker in equal amounts. Right here,
   $pH = pK_a$ ($4.76$ for acetic acid) — the dead centre of the flat
   stretch, and the point where the buffer can soak up the most added
   acid or base before pH actually moves.
5. **Buffering zone ends** — most of the $CH_3COOH$ is used up now.
   With little acid left in reserve to react with incoming $OH^-$, pH
   starts climbing faster again.
6. **Equivalence point / inflection point** (1.0 equivalents) — every
   $CH_3COOH$ molecule has now been converted to $CH_3COO^-$ — you've
   added exactly as much $OH^-$ as there was acid to begin with. The
   curve is climbing at its steepest right here. Because $CH_3COO^-$ is
   a weak base, it pulls a little $H^+$ back out of water, so the
   solution actually sits above pH $7$ — mildly basic.
7. **Endpoint** — the moment you, running the titration, actually
   notice this has happened: the indicator dye suddenly changes color,
   or the pH meter reading jumps. Ideally this lines up with step 6.
8. **Past equivalence** — keep adding $NaOH$ now and it's just excess
   strong base piling up with no more acid left to react with; pH keeps
   rising, then flattens out high, set by how much extra $NaOH$ you've
   poured in.

### Common confusion

- The **inflection point** (steepest slope) is the **equivalence point**
  — not the point where $pH = pK_a$. The point where $pH = pK_a$ is the
  **half-equivalence point**, sitting in the flat buffering zone, the
  opposite of where the curve is steepest.

### Mirror case: titrating a weak base with a strong acid

Same procedure, reversed: the titrant is a strong acid (e.g. $HCl$), the
analyte is a weak base (e.g. $NH_3$). Because acid is being added to a
base instead of base to an acid, the curve descends as titrant is added,
instead of rising.

![Titration curve of NH3 titrated by HCl: pH (0–12) plotted against mL of HCl added (0–50). The curve starts near pH 11, descends gradually through a buffering plateau around 10–12 mL where pH = pKa(NH4+) = 9.25 — the half-equivalence point — then drops steeply around 25 mL through pH 5.28, the equivalence point and inflection point, before leveling off near pH 1.4–2 as excess HCl accumulates.](/notes/biotech-eng/titration-weak-base-strong-acid.png)

| | Weak acid + strong base (acetic acid + $NaOH$) | Weak base + strong acid ($NH_3$ + $HCl$) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Titrant | $OH^-$ | $H^+$ |
| Curve direction | pH rises as titrant is added | pH falls as titrant is added |
| Species at equivalence point | conjugate base, $A^-$ | conjugate acid, $NH_4^+$ |
| pH at equivalence point | $> 7$ ($A^-$ hydrolyzes, raising pH) | $< 7$ ($NH_4^+$ hydrolyzes, lowering pH) |
| pH at half-equivalence point | $pH = pK_a$ of $HA$ | $pH = pK_a$ of $NH_4^+$ |

- Equivalence-point pH is not always $7$ — it depends on which species
  (conjugate acid or conjugate base) is left in solution and how it
  hydrolyzes.

## Buffers

- **Buffer** — an aqueous solution that resists changes to its pH when
  a small amount of acid or base is added, or when it is diluted.

### Acidic and basic buffers

| | Composition | pH |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acidic buffer | weak acid + its conjugate base | $< 7$ |
| Basic buffer | weak base + its conjugate acid | $> 7$ |

### Common physiological buffer systems

- **Phosphate buffer** — weak acid $H_2PO_4^-$, conjugate base
  $HPO_4^{2-}$.
- **Bicarbonate buffer** — weak acid $H_2CO_3$ (carbonic acid),
  conjugate base $HCO_3^-$ (bicarbonate ion):
  $H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-$. Unlike the phosphate
  buffer, $H_2CO_3$ is itself in equilibrium with dissolved $CO_2$
  ($CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3$), so the lungs (via
  breathing out $CO_2$) and the kidneys (via excreting $HCO_3^-$) can
  both adjust this buffer — it's the main buffer of blood plasma.

### What a buffer's pH range and capacity depend on

1. **Nature of the conjugate pair** — its $pK_a$ sets which pH the
   buffer is centered on.
2. **Concentrations of the pair** — the concentrations of $HA$ and
   $A^-$ set how much added acid or base the buffer can absorb.

### Conditions for an acid buffer to exist

1. The solution must contain a weak acid and its salt (the conjugate
   base).
2. The concentrations of the weak acid and its conjugate base must be
   reasonably similar.

### Mechanism — the common ion effect

Buffers operate on the **common ion effect**. The salt fully
dissociates, flooding the solution with the conjugate base ion ($A^-$)
— the same ion the weak acid's own ionization ($HA \rightleftharpoons
H^+ + A^-$) produces. That excess of $A^-$ pushes the weak acid's
equilibrium back toward $HA$ (Le Chatelier's principle), leaving both
$HA$ and $A^-$ sitting in solution at reasonably similar
concentrations, ready to react with whatever is added.

#### Worked example: acetic acid / sodium acetate buffer

**Question.** How does a mixture of acetic acid ($CH_3COOH$) and sodium
acetate ($CH_3COONa$) resist pH change when a strong acid, a strong
base, or water is added?

**Solution.**

1. Sodium acetate is a salt and dissociates completely:
   $CH_3COONa \rightarrow CH_3COO^- + Na^+$ — this supplies a large,
   standing reservoir of the conjugate base, $CH_3COO^-$.
2. Acetic acid is a weak acid, so it stays mostly undissociated:
   $CH_3COOH \rightleftharpoons CH_3COO^- + H^+$. The solution now holds
   both $CH_3COOH$ and $CH_3COO^-$ at reasonably similar concentrations
   — satisfying the conditions for a buffer.
3. **Add a strong acid** ($H^+$): the reservoir of $CH_3COO^-$ absorbs
   it — $CH_3COO^- + H^+ \rightarrow CH_3COOH$. $H^+$ is consumed
   converting conjugate base into acid, so pH barely changes.
4. **Add a strong base** ($OH^-$): the acid neutralizes it —
   $CH_3COOH + OH^- \rightarrow CH_3COO^- + H_2O$. The acid is consumed
   converting into conjugate base and water, so pH barely changes.
5. **Dilute with water**: $[CH_3COOH]$ and $[CH_3COO^-]$ both drop by
   the same factor, so their ratio is unchanged — by the
   Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, pH stays (nearly) the same.

**Answer:** Because $CH_3COOH$ and $CH_3COO^-$ are both present in
reasonably similar amounts, any added $H^+$ or $OH^-$ is consumed
converting one species into the other rather than accumulating freely
in solution, so pH shifts only slightly.

## Key points

- Atomic number = protons; mass number = protons + neutrons.
- Protium, deuterium, tritium = hydrogen's isotopes, with 0, 1, 2 neutrons.
- Water: 18 daltons, bent at 104.5°, polar. CO₂: linear, symmetrical,
  nonpolar.
- Water's unusually high mp/bp/heat of vaporization/surface tension come
  from intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
- Hydrophilic = polar = water-soluble. Hydrophobic = nonpolar =
  water-insoluble. Amphipathic = both, in one molecule.
- High dielectric constant → weaker ionic attraction → water dissolves
  salts easily.
- Water is amphoteric (acts as acid or base) and amphiprotic (donates or
  accepts H⁺): $H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-$, and
  $2H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + OH^-$. $H_3O^+$ → cathode, $OH^-$ →
  anode.
- $K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = K_{eq} \times 55.5 = 10^{-14}$ mol²/L² at 25°C
  ($K_{eq} = 1.8 \times 10^{-16}$ mol/L; $[H_2O]$ held constant at $55.5$
  mol/L).
- Pure water: $[H^+] = 10^{-7}$ mol/L, neutral. Acidic: $[H^+] > 10^{-7}$ M.
  Basic: $[H^+] < 10^{-7}$ M.
- $pH = -\log[H^+]$.
- Brønsted-Lowry: acid donates H⁺, base accepts H⁺. $HCl + H_2O
  \rightleftharpoons Cl^- + H_3O^+$ — conjugate pairs $HCl/Cl^-$ and
  $H_3O^+/H_2O$.
- Strong acids ionize completely; weak acids don't.
- $K_a = [H^+][A^-]/[HA]$ — an acid's tendency to donate its proton;
  the equilibrium constant of $HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-$.
- $pK_a = -\log K_a$. Larger $K_a$ → lower $pK_a$ → stronger acid.
  Strong acid ($HCl$): $K_a \sim 10^7$, $pK_a \sim -7$. Weak acid
  (acetic acid): $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, $pK_a = 4.76$.
- Henderson-Hasselbalch: $pH = pK_a + \log([A^-]/[HA])$. When
  $[A^-] = [HA]$, $pH = pK_a$ — the acid is 50% dissociated, the
  buffer's point of maximum capacity.
- Titration: titrant (known conc.) added to analyte (unknown conc.)
  until the reaction is complete. A weak acid is titrated with a strong
  base; the titration curve plots pH vs. $OH^-$ added.
- Equivalence point = inflection point (steepest slope): moles titrant
  added = moles acid originally present. Half-equivalence point
  ($[HA]=[A^-]$, $pH=pK_a$) sits in the flat buffering zone instead —
  the two are easily confused but are opposite points on the curve.
- Curve order (weak acid + strong base, as $OH^-$ is added): start
  (low pH) → early rise → buffering zone begins → half-equivalence
  ($pH=pK_a$) → buffering zone ends → equivalence/inflection point
  ($pH>7$, endpoint detected here) → past equivalence, pH levels off.
- Mirror case: weak base + strong acid ($NH_3$ + $HCl$) — curve falls
  instead of rises; equivalence-point pH is $< 7$ (conjugate acid
  $NH_4^+$ hydrolyzes) instead of $> 7$.
- Buffer = resists pH change on adding acid/base or on dilution.
  Acidic buffer (weak acid + conjugate base), $pH<7$; basic buffer
  (weak base + conjugate acid), $pH>7$. Physiological examples:
  phosphate buffer ($H_2PO_4^-/HPO_4^{2-}$), bicarbonate buffer
  ($H_2CO_3/HCO_3^-$ — blood's main buffer, tunable by lungs and
  kidneys).
- Buffer range/capacity depend on the conjugate pair's $pK_a$ and on
  their concentrations. Requires a weak acid + its salt, at reasonably
  similar concentrations — works via the common ion effect (excess
  $A^-$ from the salt suppresses the acid's own ionization).
- Acetic acid/sodium acetate buffer: $CH_3COO^-$ (from the fully
  dissociated salt) mops up added $H^+$; $CH_3COOH$ mops up added
  $OH^-$; dilution leaves the $[A^-]/[HA]$ ratio — and so the pH —
  unchanged.
