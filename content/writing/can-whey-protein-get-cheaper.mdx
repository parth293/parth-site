An Indian supplement brand recently [published its own raw material
costs](https://onlywhatsneeded.in/engage/whey-tracker): ₹1,475 per kg of raw
whey in July 2025, ₹3,700 per kg by June 2026. Just over a doubling, most of
it packed into the last three months of that window. Their fully-loaded
selling price is heading to ₹4,500–5,000 per kg to match.

I have no reason to doubt the numbers, and the four causes they list are all
real: GLP-1 drugs pushing protein demand up sharply, whey supply that cannot
grow without more cheese production growing with it, protein going mainstream
beyond athletes, and India importing almost all its whey in dollars and euros
while the rupee weakens. What I want to work through is the question their
post doesn't ask, because it isn't the brand's job to ask it: is ₹3,700 a
price the market is stuck with, or is some of it slack that better process
and supply design could take out?

## The part that isn't a lever

Start with the constraint that rules a few ideas out immediately. Whey is not
a product a dairy sets out to make. It's the liquid left over when milk is
curdled to pull out casein for cheese or paneer, and it only exists because
that curdling happened. You cannot tell a dairy to "make more whey" the way
you'd tell a fab to run another shift — the volume of whey is fixed the
moment the volume of cheese is fixed. So "increase supply" only works by
increasing cheese and paneer output for its own reasons, with whey riding
along as a co-product. That's a decade-scale dairy-industry shift, not
something a protein brand or a process engineer changes this year.

That rules out the most obvious lever. It leaves three that are real:
capturing whey that already exists but isn't being processed, cutting the
cost of turning liquid whey into powder, and substituting a different protein
source for some of the volume.

## Capturing whey that already gets thrown away

India makes an enormous amount of paneer and chhena, and both are made by
curdling milk exactly like cheese is — with a whey stream as the byproduct.
The difference is that a cheese plant is usually built to also run
ultrafiltration and spray-drying on its whey, because it was designed as an
industrial dairy operation from the start. A huge share of India's paneer is
made by small local producers with none of that downstream equipment, so the
whey goes down the drain instead of into a dryer.

I don't have a real number for what fraction of Indian paneer whey is
captured versus wasted — I'd guess it's low outside of large organized
dairies, but that's a guess, not a figure I can defend. If it's genuinely
low, this is the closest thing to free supply in the whole system: the milk
has already been paid for and processed once, and the whey is a sunk
byproduct rather than a new input you have to source and pay for at
whatever price the global market sets. The catch is capital. Ultrafiltration
membranes plus a spray dryer are a real plant, not an add-on, so this only
pencils out at a scale bigger than most paneer producers run at — which
argues for aggregation (a central UF/drying facility multiple small paneer
makers sell liquid whey into) rather than every producer building their own.

## Cutting the cost of the process, not the input

Assume the whey volume is fixed. There's still a processing cost stacked on
top of the raw whey price, and it isn't free of slack either. Two spots stand
out:

- **Evaporation before spray drying** is the single biggest energy draw in
  the whole line, because you're boiling most of the water out of a dilute
  stream before you can dry it. Mechanical vapour recompression reuses the
  vapour's own heat instead of venting it, and retrofitting it onto an
  existing evaporator is a real, known lever for cutting that energy cost —
  not a moonshot, just capital most Indian mid-size plants haven't spent yet.
- **Yield loss in ultrafiltration and diafiltration** — protein that ends up
  fouling the membrane or leaving in the permeate instead of the retentate —
  is money that was already spent on raw whey and then lost during
  processing. Every percentage point of yield recovered is a percentage
  point less raw whey you need to buy for the same output.

Neither of these touches the ₹3,700 figure. Both reduce how much of that
₹3,700 you need per kg of finished powder, which is the same thing from a
selling-price standpoint.

## Substitution — where the ethical line actually is

The brand's post draws a hard line against "cheap amino acids to inflate the
protein number" and quiet reformulation, and that line is correct — spiking
a protein test with free amino acids to game the number on the label is
fraud, not cost engineering. But there's a real technique on the other side
of that line: blending declared, tested amounts of a cheaper complete
protein — soy or pea isolate, milk protein concentrate — into a labeled
blend product sold as a separate, cheaper SKU next to the pure-whey one. That
isn't hiding anything; it's giving a price-sensitive customer an honest
choice instead of one option at whatever price pure whey now costs.

The other substitution path is further out: whey protein made by
fermentation rather than by cows, where an engineered yeast or fungal strain
is grown to express the same milk proteins directly. Perfect Day is the name
most associated with this. It decouples protein supply from cheese output
entirely, which is the actual fix for the co-product constraint above. I
don't know where that technology's production cost sits today relative to
dairy whey, and I'd be guessing if I gave a number — the honest state of that
lever is that it exists and matters directionally, not that it's cost
competitive yet.

## What I'd want to know next

The whole domestic-capture argument stands or falls on one number I don't
have: what share of India's paneer and chhena whey is actually going
uncaptured right now. If it's a rounding error, the aggregation model in the
second section isn't worth building. If it's large, it's the cheapest lever
on this list, because the milk is already paid for. That's the number that
would change how much weight I put on this piece.
